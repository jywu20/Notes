\chapter{Introduction}

\section{The language}

This note is about Classical Chinese,
often known natively (in Mandarin Chinese) as 文言 (\translate{lit. cultured speech}).
Regarding the meaning of the relevant terms see \prettyref{sec:introduction.golden-bronze}.

The language is sometimes known as \form{Wen-li} by Western missionaries,
especially in Bible translation.
This seems to be a misunderstanding of the word 文理,
which is a nominal compound and means rhetorics (i.e. 文) and meanings (i.e. 理) of literature works.
An educated person therefore would be described as ``通文理'' (\translate{fluent in rhetorics and meanings}).
Such a person of course would have decent understanding of Classical Chinese,
and hence 文理 was probably mistranslated as ``Classical Chinese'',
although the word was not natively used to refer to the latter \citep[\citepage{161}]{zetzsche2017bible}.

Classical Chinese is also sometimes known as 古文 (\translate{lit. ancient articles})
or 古汉语 (\translate{lit. ancient Chinese}),
and equated with late Old Chinese.
This terminology may be misleading, 
as there was considerable diachronic and regional variances in Old Chinese (\prettyref{sec:introduction.text}).

Further, ``old'' written materials were definitely \emph{not} always written in 文言. 
Novels like \work{Journey to the West} or \work{Dream of the Red Chamber} were written in earlier forms of Mandarin.
Buddhist scriptures translated to Chinese are closer to the Classical paradigm
but are also definitely influenced by the grammar of Middle Chinese.
The non-Classical written materials, despite being important both culturally and linguistically,
are beyond the scope of this work.

\subsection{Classical Chinese vs. Literary Chinese}\label{sec:introduction.golden-bronze}

The word 文言 is more accurately translated to \term{Literary Chinese} in English.
Strictly speaking, we need to distinguish between \term{Literary} Chinese,
i.e. the high variety of the more than one millennia of diglossia in China,
and \term{Classical} Chinese,
i.e. the language of prose texts considered paradigmatic and having the prescriptive authority to dictate the ``right'' usages of Literary Chinese.

A natural question is then whether it is in fact meaningful to talk about a single, largely homogeneous \term{Classical Chinese},
when the Classical texts admittedly have diverse features.

The goal of this grammar is to analyze the shared grammatical features of these texts.
This resembles the stated goal of \citet{cgel},
which is to analyze \term{International Standard English},
i.e. the shared core of all important contemporary varieties of English. 
The existence of such a shared Classical grammatical core should not be taken for granted
and can only be proven by demonstrating that most texts considered Classical are analyzable under the framework of this work (see also \prettyref{sec:introduction.history.han}).

A subsequent question is whether such an enterprise is even theoretically sound:
there always is a possibility that the received Classical texts are heavily distorted in their transmission and artificially normalized at a certain juncture.


\section{History, texts and scope}\label{sec:introduction.text}


\subsection{Scope of this grammar}

We cover both pre-Han and Han texts in this grammar.
This includes the received Classical texts (TODO: table) 
Excavated texts are invaluable but existing excavated texts often have dialectological issues 
(\prettyref{sec:introduction.text.pre-han.dialect}).

An overview of the post-Zhou history of Chinese language(s) is given in the next several sections,
in which we also justify the scope of this grammar.

Literary Chinese, i.e. 文言 in its broad sense,
also includes post-Classical texts whose grammars are roughly based on the Old Chinese canon
but may have a variety of innovations (\prettyref{sec:introduction.history.post-han}).
We do note cite examples from post-Classical texts, except when illustrating diachronic points.
The situation here precisely resembles the case of Latin,
in which we have Classical Latin and Medieval Latin which imitates the former. 

\subsection{Late Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods}\label{sec:intro.sprint-and-autumn}

\subsubsection{Periodization}

The early history of Chinese is commonly divided into 
an Old Chinese period and the Middle Chinese period.
The criteria of the division is given in \prettyref{sec:introduction.history.post-han}.
The transition between the two periods is typically considered somewhere near the end of the Eastern Han dynasty.
Thus, the Old Chinese period contains Shang and Western Zhou, 
the Spring and Autumn period which filled with chaotic (but not intense) wars between numerous dukedoms,
the Warring States period which observed intense wars between seven major states, and the rise and fall of the Qin Empire and finally the Han dynasty.

The Old Chinese period can be divided into the pre-Classical and Classical periods.
We consider most texts before Confucius pre-Classical,
even considering that some of them have classic status in Confucianism.
Thus the Classical period, in the broad sense, spans from the late Spring and Autumn period
to late Han dynasty.
In a narrower sense it properly begins with Confucius and ends at the founding of the Qin empire (\citealt[\citepage{3}]{pulleyblank1995outline}; \prettyref{sec:intro.sprint-and-autumn}).
The Han dynasty witnessed the unification and likely koineization towards a common literary standard (\citealt[\citepages{3-4}]{pulleyblank1995outline}; \prettyref{sec:introduction.history.han}).

We justify the given pre-Classical/Classical boundary below.

First, pre-Spring and Autumn texts are certainly pre-Classical.

This treatment is consistent with traditional philological scholarship in China:
Han Yu, renowned for reviving Classical prose, famously described the \work{Book of Documents} as 诘屈聱牙 (\translate{twisted, hard to pronounce}) in his 进学解 (\work{Analysis of academic advancement}).
The \work{Book of Change} similarly is not comprehensible to a scholar trained only in Classical Chinese and requires commentaries to be understood.
The \work{Book of Odes} includes poems spanning a long period of time from early Zhou to Spring and Autumn with considerable regional differences.%
\footnote{
    This may partly be due to the nature of poetry:
    regularization in transmission is much easier for prose than for it is for poetry.
}
These texts are not imitated by post-Classical prose writers, except in e,g, imperial proclamations.

Both lexical and grammatical differences can be spotted between Zhou and later texts (received or excavated).
The copula 惟 is out of use in Classical texts 
and the copula construction had been largely replaced by the nominal predication construction
(\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.nominal}).
The conjunction 而, uniforming appearing in all later texts, received or excavated, is never seen in pre-Spring and Autumn texts.
The pronoun 厥 and the verb 克 (meaning \translate{be able to}) are largely lacking in later texts.

Second, early Spring and Autumn texts are also not a part of the Classical canon.

Limited texts before Confucius are either traditionally passed down in the \work{Odes},
or are attested in bronze inscriptions archaeologically discovered,
and are not imitated by post-Classical prose writers.
There lacks a large corpus of early Spring and Autumn continuous prose comparable to, say, the Confucian classics. 
The bronze inscriptions strongly resemble the Zhou paradigm and are thus pre-Classical.%
\footnote{
    As in 秦公钟: 余小子……克明厥心……
}



\subsubsection{Dialectology}\label{sec:introduction.text.pre-han.dialect}

A western/eastern dialectal differences can be observed within the \work{Odes} \citep{ma2025ji}
and thus it is not unreasonable to expect regional variances during the Classical period.

A rough tripartite dialectal division between the western Qin and Jin dialects, the eastern Qi and Lu dialects, and the southern Chu dialects can be observed in this period,
based on evidence from phonology \citep{bian2017dialect,wang2000dialect}, sentence-final particles \citep{miyajima2024sfp}, conjunction particles, prepositions, and clause embedding \citep{onishi1998conj}.
Among Warring States texts, the \work{Mencius} explicitly mentions strong dialectal difference between Qi and Chu dialects (一齐众楚). 
Admixture is not uncommon \citep{bian2017dialect} and thus this division is currently still a tentative dialect-geographical abstraction, and is not necessarily a family tree in the neogrammarian sense.

The most salient grammatical features of Qin dialect are not present in later prose.
As an example, the sentence final particle 殹 never appears in later formal prose.
Similarly, the Chu dialect also had limited contributions to Classical prose.
This is indicated by the fact that most Chu-specific words are nowhere to be found in Classical texts.%
\footnote{
    We should however distinguish grammatical variances from orthographical variances (e.g. the ones in \citealt{wu2011chucausative}).
}

It however is premature to conclude that Classical texts were based on the eastern dialect group.
The main problem is that there is currently no substantial excavated Warring States Qi or Lu bamboo manuscript corpus:
the Qin and Chu languages are attested directly in excavated texts, while Qi/Lu dialects are not and we know ``extremely little'' about their written forms \citep{feng2024some}.
No excavated pre-Han texts are included to represent eastern dialects in \citet{bian2017dialect}:
their sample instead include \work{Zuozhuan}, \work{Xunzi}, and Han bamboo manuscripts from Yinqueshan 银雀山, and \work{Odes} poems thought to represent eastern dialects.

The currently available \work{Analect} text were widely considered to be from Lu (i.e. 魯論語) and presumably contains eastern dialectal features,
and the \work{Mencius} largely resembles the \work{Analect} grammatically \citep[\citepages{63-77,90}]{karlgren1936zuozhuan}.
The shared grammatical features between the works may be attributed to the shared language of early Confucianism,
but the possibility of later regularization cannot be outright ruled out. 

On the other hand, despite being traditionally attributed to Lu historians and included into the eastern group in \citet{bian2017dialect} in their comparative study,
the language of \work{Zuozhuan} is well known to be different from that of the \work{Analect} in terms of the syntax of prepositions, conditional markers, and demonstratives \citep[\citepages{63-77}]{karlgren1936zuozhuan}.

This may indicate existence of yet another dialect, possibly a pre-Han koine used by historians \citep[\citepage{3}]{pulleyblank1995outline}. 
Alternatively, some of the diverging behaviors observed by Karlgren, like choices of prepositions, may just be due to historical development \citep{venture2020preposition}.
We should also keep in mind that received texts are layered and often have a rather complex textual history.

Slightly later texts, include \work{Zhuangzi}, \work{Xunzi} and \work{Han Feizi}, have largely comparable grammatical features with resemblance to both the Lu dialect and the language of \work{Zuozhuan} \citep[\citepages{92-93}]{karlgren1936zuozhuan}.
The exact nature of the similarities and differences between these texts, the \work{Analect} and \form{Zuozhuan} again has multiple possibilities, ranging from
convergence towards a common literary koine \citep[\citepage{3}]{pulleyblank1995outline}
to retaining of weakening archaism in the same dialectal tradition;
later artificial normalization also cannot be confidently ruled out.

In conclusion, we can safety state that the Qin and Chu dialects attested in excavated texts have marked differences from the Classical paradigm, even though they were written in the Classical period.
Determining the exact origin of the Classical paradigm known today however is much harder.
On one hand, there is evidence suggesting a disproportionate large eastern contribution to later Classical prose:
the idiosyncratic features of Qin and Chu do not appear in later prose; 
the traditional narratives attribute the \work{Analect} and \work{Zuozhuan} and related texts to Lu;
there is phonological evidence for them being in a eastern dialect group.
On the other hand, the internal diversity of received texts from the Classical period cannot ignored,
which has multiple explanations,
and the lack of excavated eastern texts make it a shaky practice to call Classical texts eastern without qualification.%
\footnote{
    It would be a major breakthrough to rediscover a corpus of Lu texts.
    Our eastern-dominated model of received Classical texts would face a major challenge 
    if documentary or scholarly manuscripts written in eastern dialects were discovered and demonstrated to be markedly different from received texts.
    In this case, the formation of the Classical standard would need to be postponed to the Han dynasty koineization and canonization.

    Interestingly and unfortunately, manuscripts that were highly likely from Lu were once found from the walls of Confucius's old residence in Qufu during the last half of the 2nd century BC, and later lost.
    汉书·艺文志 records Liu Xiang's textual criticism of the excavated texts,
    observing $\sim 700$ textual variants. 
    The received \work{Analect} text, edited by 郑玄 in the Eastern Han dynasty and was known to be partly based on these archaic texts (but with no received critical apparatus).
    郑玄 also made a critical apparatus of 仪礼, which contains largely differences in content words and orthography.

    Evidence listed above is consistent with the hypothesis that the texts from the walls (presumably in eastern dialects) had no substantial linguistic differences from received texts,
    and hence our model that the Classical paradigm was predominantly shaped by eastern dialects.
    But the evidence is also consistent with the later Han koineization model:
    it may be the case that Zheng Xuan intentionally ignored all grammatical variances he observed.
}

A final issue is that students of Confucius noticed that when he recited texts he considered classics and presided rituals,
he used 雅言 or \translate{elegant speech} (\work{Analects} 7:18).
One may wonder what this elegant speech exactly was (presumably it should have Zhou components),
but due to lack of materials, it is hard to pin down its source.

\subsection{The Han dynasty}\label{sec:introduction.history.han}

\subsubsection{Emergence of a literary koine}\label{sec:introduction.history.han.prose}

The last batch of works that may be reasonably considered Classical were composed during Han dynasty,
among which is \work{Shiji} 史记 (\translate{lit. historical records}),
also known as 太史公记 \form{Records of the Grand Historian},
which laid the paradigm of official historiography of all Chinese dynasties after Han.

The dialectal distinctions in lexicon or grammar discussed in the last section are largely absent in the most prestigious Han prose texts (\citealt[\citepage{94}]{karlgren1936zuozhuan}; see also \prettyref{sec:introduction.history.han.dialect}).

The overall grammar of prestigious Han prose is consistent with pre-Qin Classical texts.
For instance, \citet{he2005shiji} is a comprehensive comparison between \work{Shiji} and \work{Zuozhuan},
which finds notable but largely quantitative differences, mainly an increase of complex forms in the texts.
Two noticeable possibly qualitative developments are more pre-verbal prepositional constituents,
and more forms that can be analyzed as verbal complements (补语),
which are likely natural developments towards Middle Chinese.
The change was therefore not as radical as the change 
from the \form{Documents} to Spring and Autumn texts
but also noticeable enough to justify separating Han as a unique linguistic period from the Warring State period 
\citep[\citepages{260-264}]{he2005shiji}.

However, Han prose should not be regarded as a simple continuation of the proposed eastern Classical standard.
The short-lived Qin dynasty substantially shaped the standard of \emph{writing}, and likely also phonology of at least some dialects.
\citet{bian2017dialect} for instance classify loangraphs in \work{Shiji} as evidence of western dialects,
which can be interpreted as a reflection of the phonology of Sima Qian's own language or the writing standard in his time.
On the other hand, as is said above, Qin influence on Classical prose is much more modest in grammar.
One clear influence of the language of Qin on \work{Shiji} is the comitative-only usage of 与 \citep{onishi1998conj}.

The lexicon of Western Han prose shows much stronger admixture (TODO: 周秦两汉楚方言词研究).

What we observe above appears to be significant koineization during the Western Han dynasty.
If Classical Chinese is taken to be linguistically realistic in a certain period,
we may thus identify it with this literary koine emerging in the Western Han.
Its overall grammar and lexicon are largely based on the emerging common elite prose standard attested in linguistically converging late Warring States texts,
possibly originating from eastern dialects 
(see caveats in \prettyref{sec:introduction.text.pre-han.dialect}).
It has also received lexical influences from other dialects.
Its phonology and orthography, on the other hand, are likely strongly influenced by the \emph{western} dialects.

The final formation of such a koine is presumably partly a consequence of mass destruction of non-Legalism texts during the Qin dynasty (though the nature and scale of the destruction has been long debated):
surviving texts, regardless of their dialectal origins, were revered as the golden standard of prose, and a great admixture of grammatical features thus happened \citep[\citepage{94}]{karlgren1936zuozhuan}.

Another factor is institutional.
The Han Empire had a centralized system of governance and actively sought ancient texts and appointed erudites to profess Confucian classics; an obvious but not overly strong preference for scholars from the Qi-Lu area can be seen \citep{cai2019han}.
This created an ideal environment for koineization.
Presumably, the existing Qi-Lu scholarship tradition and its elite language was strengthened by its incorporation into Han officialdom,
and western (recall that the capital city of Han, Changan, was in the former Qin territory) and southern scholars were absorbed into the network,
bringing some but not all of their regional features into the koine.

The Han dynasty also had a national education system for future officials.
Excavated legal documents describe orthographic training and examinations in handwriting and recitation of texts \citep{li2007scribe}.

\subsubsection{Dialectology and genres}\label{sec:introduction.history.han.dialect}

The literary koine for prose does not cover the whole of the language situation of the Han dynasty.
Lexically, this has already been clearly indicated by Yang Xiong's 方言,
and in this section we discuss the phonological and grammatical aspects of Han dynasty dialectology. 
Relevant topics are beyond the scope of this grammar and here we can merely skim through them to illustrate the nature of the Classical paradigm.

First, literature in the Han dynasty is not limited to prose.
Especially worth noticing is the \form{fu} genre.
Traditionally, \form{fu} is believed to originate from Chu poems,
for the obvious reason that Chu words appear frequently in \form{fu}.
The \form{fu} genre also likely involved koineization,
given the tendency for \form{fu} authors to use rare words and their diverse regional origins.

A natural question is whether the koine of \form{fu} differs in a meaningful way from the koine of Han prose. The grammar of \form{fu} is beyond the scope of this grammar.


Second, the rhyme pattern of poems may indicate dialectal phonological differences of the oral language(s),
though it is well known that rhyme patterns are often also strongly influenced by the literary tradition.
Dialectal differences, largely consistent with Yang Xiong's 方言, can indeed be observed \citep{zhang2023rhyme}.
Obviously, the literary koine was not always identical to the phonology of oral speeches.

Third, we may also wonder the overall difference between the prose koine and the oral language(s) of the period.
This also involves grammatical continuation of the second caveat:
certain types of excavated Han manuscripts are often assumed to better reflect the oral language(s),
and thus inconsistencies between the prose koine and the oral language(s) may appear and have regional variances.

The excavated administrative texts and private letters do possess certain divergences from the Classical paradigm.
\citet{ren2022robbery} notices a possible possessive pronoun 央 attested in contemporary southern Sinitic languages but not in Classical Chinese
and also a dialectal term recorded by Yang Xiong.
The overall grammatical framework however largely resembles the Classical paradigm,
once orthographical inconsistencies (e.g. 得毋 vs. 得无) are resolved.
Some seemingly unusual usages, like person names followed by 辞 before long narratives,
can be reasonable explained within the Classical framework (in this case, a noun taking a preceding clause about the interrogation) \citep{ren2022robbery}. 

This can be perceived as evidence for the Classical prose paradigm being the literary language of not only elite scholars but also low-level officials and scribes (see the end of \prettyref{sec:introduction.history.han.prose}).
It remains to be seen if a systematic classification of grammatical peculiarities in these documents is possible, if there is any.

\subsection{On the status of post-Han texts}\label{sec:introduction.history.post-han}

The end of Old Chinese -- and hence the end of the Classical period --
is marked by, among others, 
the emergence of 是 as a copula (and not just a demonstrative),
the appearance of the disposal construction 
and the long passive construction  \citep[\citepage{35}]{wang2013history}.

Thus the mental grammar of an author in post-Han Literary Chinese prose almost certainly differed from any possible grammar of Classical writers.
Expectedly, despite purification attempts,
vernacular elements made their ways into not only administrative documents
but also pure literature and scholar works.

A radical example is texts from the Republican period of modern Chinese history, 
when official documents were written largely in a pseudo-Classical style:
the abstract syntax behind these documents is almost certainly within the framework of modern Mandarin,
with the possessive marker 的 replaced by 之
and the relative marker 的 in headless relative clauses replaced by 者 (不服管教的 \translate{who does not conform to instructions} is replaced by 不服管教者).
What is Classical in these documents is primarily the superficial forms.

Still, because of the highly formulaic nature of these documents (for instance, usually no sentence final particle appears, and valency alternation besides the passive is discouraged),
readers of these texts still benefit from studying the grammar of Classical Chinese.
More details can be found in \prettyref{sec:genres.pseudo}.

\subsubsection{Decay and revival of Classical prose}

The fall of the Han Empire was accompanied by the fall of Classical prose except in historiography.
The post-Han literature history witnessed proliferation of \work{Fu} and later the emergence of \work{pianwen},
and the emergence of Chinese translations of Buddhist texts (which retains a lot of Classical features but already contains colloquial features) and later the 变文 genre (containing strongly oral features).

Literary Chinese, as we know it today (\prettyref{sec:introduction.golden-bronze}), can be attributed to the mid-Tang Classical Prose Movement.
Proponents of Classical prose, the most famous ones being Han Yu, Liu Zongyuan, and the rest of the Eight Masters of the Tang and Song,
rejecting \work{pianwen} which they considered overly decorative and empty in substance, 
strongly advocated for a return to both Classical prose and the Old Way, i.e. Confucian ideals.

The movement was incredibly successfully and successfully redefined what prose --- 文 --- meant for subsequent generations.
Thus, we can say that despite that the Classical prose paradigm had stabilizedin the Han dynasty,
the concept of a Literary Chinese, 
independent of oral Sinitic languages, 
with its prescriptive authority being the Classical texts, 
competent of writing about everything from history to epitaph,
was not in place until the Classical Prose Movement.

Literary Chinese inevitably has vernacular influences from all ages.
Its diachronic development is not covered in this grammar.
Its existence, however, greatly strengths the cultural importance of Classical Chinese.

\subsection{Native grammatical traditions}\label{sec:introduction.previous.tradition}

Classical Chinese authors had conversations about grammaticality 
and uses of grammatical particles
reminiscent of how English native speakers
with some exposure to the study of English grammar but no formal training:
``delete the \form{the} here and your sentence looks more concise''.
No attempts were made to establish intermediate concepts between words and utterances,
like structural templates of phrases or grammatical relations, 
and to organize the grammar as a machine producing acceptable utterances:
discussions on grammatical topics were either for education or for rhetorics.

The grammatical awareness of Classical Chinese authors was somehow comparable to 
what an ancient Roman grammarian or \form{grammaticus} did,
who set his main role as a secondary educator,
refrained from analyzing some sort of ``underlying'' or ``internalized'' system behind the surface forms
and was satisfied by mostly surface-oriented patterns,
and would not set up any intermediate concepts between the word and the utterance
\citep[\citepages{7,35,47-48}]{matthews2019graeco}.

\citep{kiparsky2009architecture}.

\subsection{The grammar of characters?}

A misconception commonly found among people not well informed about sinology
is that Chinese characters directly represent ideas.
This is wrong.
However, a question worth asking is whether the complex and compositional structure of Chinese characters resembles the architecture of spoken natural languages.

This issue has been explored in \citet{myers2019grammar},
with a largely affirmative answer:
in the book he identifies a word-like grammar (consisting of not only morphology but also phonology) of Chinese characters,%
\footnote{
    Worth noticing is that similar patterns can be found in all orthographic systems \citep[\citepages{23-25}]{myers2019grammar}.
    English orthography, for instance, has phonology-like rules like \form{-y} + suffix \textto{}  \form{-i}-suffix \citep[\citepage{26}]{myers2019grammar}.
}
and that these grammar-like rules are likely still active even today.

\chapter{Grammatical overview}\label{chap:grammatical}


In this chapter we summarize the syntax of the clause (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause}) and the syntax of the noun phrase (NP) (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.np}), along two intertwined lines:
systems and constructions in clauses and noun phrases and grammatical functions therein, 
and lexical properties that determine how forms appear in these environments.

Based on distribution of forms in these environments, parts of speech, i.e. \emph{lexical} form classes, can be defined:
thus, a form being from the noun class indicates that it has a strong nominal distributional preference.
The mapping between form classes in the lexicon and grammatical environments is not always clear.%
\footnote{
    An example is that in Maya languages, there are root classes 
    from which grammatical words are regularly built
    \citep[\citepage{372}]{siddiqi2020routledge}.
}
We discuss parts of speech division in \prettyref{sec:grammatical.pos}.


\section{Morphological typology}\label{sec:grammatical.morphology}

Grammatical wordhood has been a controversial topic in linguistics.
Because Classical Chinese has no inflectional morphology,
it is not easy to demarcate word boundaries.
Indirect evidence from phonology, like the criterion of stress domain
\citep[\citepages{41-42}]{zhudexigrammar},
is obviously not available for a dead language with little phonological recording.

Whether a composite form is a word cannot be easily seen from its structure building process,
which often resembles phrasal syntax in modern Sinitic languages
\citep[\citepage{41}]{zhudexigrammar}.%
\footnote{
    \citet[\citepages{41-43}]{zhudexigrammar} gives three other criteria besides the criterion of stress:
    (a) only words contain bound forms, (b) branches of a composite word cannot be modified, and (c) composite words have unpredictable meanings.
    As is admitted by Zhu, phenomena related to criterion (c) can be explained by idiomization.
    Criterion (a) is circular as it is repeating the definition of bound form:
    a verb obligatorily needs an argument to complement, but it does not make it a bound form.
    Criterion (b) phenomena can be simply explained by assuming that a \emph{different kind of modification} is needed:
    consider \form{[ethno-[methodology]]-ical}, which structurally resembles phrases with modification.
    Without inflectional or phonological criteria,
    it is hard to explain how this form is fundamentally different from prototypical noun phrases.
}
It is hard to determine if such word-internal processes were already there during the Classical period.

One strongly, \emph{morphology-based} indication of wordhood in Classical Chinese is AABB reduplication:
phrasal or syntactic reduplication, already rare in comparison to morphological reduplication, 
tends to produce ABAB forms.
These forms therefore are likely morphological reduplication \citep{travis2001syntax}.

An often ignored indirect criterion of wordhood is \emph{grammatical} lexical specification:%
\footnote{
    Terms like \term{lexical specification} or \term{lexicalization} are used to refer to many different though related phenomena \citep{harley1999distributed}.
    In this work, (\emph{grammatical}) lexical specification means morphosyntactic listedness, i.e. a form having a lexicalized specified grammatical behavior.
    This is different from idiomization (semantic lexicalization, i.e. a form having a non-compositional semantic reading) or collapse of the internal structure of a form to a simpler one.
}
an (phrasal) idiom (like \form{kick the bucket}) also has a single, coherent meaning, 
but it is much less common for a phrasal idiom to also impose strong constraints to its complementation and modification.
Thus the Mandarin verb 關心, despite having a regular verb-object structure,
is a word, as it being transitive cannot be regularly inferred and thus has to be lexically specified.%
\footnote{
    Which, in this case, is subcategorization. 
}
In this way, when the internal makeup of clauses and noun phrases depends on lexical properties of the head verb/noun, the heads are grammatical words.

\section{Clause}\label{sec:grammatical.clause}

Like all other languages, a Classical Chinese clause can be a simple clause
or a complex one constructed from subordination (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.linking})
and coordination (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.coordination}).

A simple Classical Chinese clause is a nucleus clause plus discourse-related devices,
including its speech act marked by sentence final particles (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.force}), 
and topicalization or focalization (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.information}).
Topicalization can also happen for a complex clause (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.coordination.topic-chain}).
These discourse-related devices typically only appear in sentential clauses and are absent in embedded clauses (except for direct quotations; \prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.complement-clause}). 

A nucleus clause may have nominal (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.nominal}) or verbal predication (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal}).
We call it a \term{nominal clause}\footnote{We do not use the term to refer to a nominalized clause or a complement clause (which often does not have many nominal features anyway).} or a \term{verb clause}, respectively.
Both constructions seem to have a well-defined subject position (which is not the same as topic),
which however is often left blank (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.subject}).
Classical Chinese lacks obligatory \ac{tam} marking but does have a \ac{tam} system marked by adverbs and possibly some sentence final particles (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.tam}).



\subsection{Nominal predication}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.nominal}


The top-level structure of a Classical Chinese clause may contain 
an optional subject and a \ac{np} acting as the predicate
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.nominal.isa.1},
\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.nominal.havea.1}).
A nominal predicate typically expresses an ``is-a'' relation between the subject (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.subject.clause-pivot}) and the predicate
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.nominal.isa.1}).
Some nominal clauses however express a possessive relation between the two 
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.nominal.havea.1}).




\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.nominal.isa.1}
    \glll
    [此 人]_{\text{subject}} [力士]_{\text{predicate:NP}} \\
    cǐ rén lì-shì \\
    this person strength-man \\
    \glt\translate{This person is a man of great strength.} \hfill (史記·魏公子列傳)

    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.nominal.havea.1}
    \glll
    [蟹]_{\text{subject}} [六 跪 而 二 螯]_{\text{predicate:NP}} \\
    xiè liù guì ér èr áo \\
    crab six leg \category{conj} two claw \\
    \glt\translate{A crab has six legs and two claws.} \hfill (荀子·勸學)
\end{exe}

We note that the nominal predication is nominal in the structural sense:
a verbal clause can have a predicate headed by a form only recorded as a noun in the lexicon (cf. \prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.word}).

Negation in Classical Chinese nominal clauses is usually expressed by 非,
placed before the nominal predicate (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.nominal.is-not.1}).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.nominal.is-not.1}
    \glll
    [[非]_{\text{negation}} 忠臣]_{\text{predicate:NP}} 也 \\
    fēi zhōng-chén yě \\
    \category{neg} loyal-minister \category{sfp} \\
    \glt\translate{[He] is not a loyal minister.} \hfill (說苑·立節)
\end{exe}

The nominal predicate is compatible with \ac{tam} adverbs (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.nominal.tam.1}).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.nominal.tam.1}
    \glll
    且 相如 [素]_{\text{TAM}} 賤人 \\
    qiě Xiàngrú sù jiàn-rén \\
    and \category{name} always humble-person \\
    \glt\translate{\dots and Lin Xiangru has always been a person with a very low social status.} \hfill (史記·廉頗藺相如列傳)
\end{exe}





\subsubsection{Copula constructions} 

All the constructions mentioned above are without a copula.
In the pre-Classical age there was a copula 惟,
which however had largely died out of use in Classical texts.
Meanwhile, grammaticalization had added several copulas to Classical Chinese
\citep[\citepages{20-22}]{pulleyblank1995outline}.

\subsection{Verbal predication}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal}

In describing clausal arguments, it is wise to separate (deep) slots in argument structure and (surface) grammatical relations.
In morphologically richer languages, the mapping from these two to surface morphological cases may also be heterogeneous \citep{maling2001dative};
Classical Chinese has no case system and does not have quirky subjects marked by adpositions.

We use the standard S, A, P, G, T etc. labels to refer to argument slots,
without any implication of unity of morphosyntactic properties of arguments slots represented by a single label (S roles famously split into A-like and O-like ones, for instance).
Some argument slots have no letter labels and are represented as \term{location} or \term{instrument} etc. 

The mapping from the argument slots to grammatical functions and surface morphological case marking is known as alignment.
The diagnostics below supports treating Classical Chinese as having nominative-accusative alignment and hence has a well-defined subject
(\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.subject}).
A verbal clause consists of a subject and a verb phrase (VP), which is the predicate of the clause and contains \ac{tam} adverbs and core and peripheral arguments.
Verb phrases can be coordinated (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.coordination}), demonstrating their constituent status.
The subject, being outside the verb phrase, is sometimes known as the external argument in the literature \citep[\citepage{53}]{cgel}.

On the other hand, it is cross-linguistically harder to establish the \term{object} as a recurring grammatical function across different clausal constructions (cf. \citealt[\citepages{28-29}]{Pinkster1}).
The term \term{object} is used to refer to any argument without an explicit preposition for convenience \citep[\citepage{102}]{li2004grammar}. 

When \ac{tam} adverbs and peripheral arguments both appear before the verb,
the order is always \ac{tam} \before peripheral argument (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.order-of-modifier.1}).
The reverse order is never attested.
The verb phrase therefore can be analyzed as the (extended) argument structure
plus \ac{tam} adverbs preceding the pre-verbal peripheral arguments.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.order-of-modifier.1}
    \glll
    [三王]_{\text{subject}} [[既]_{\text{\ac{tam}}} [以 ---]_{\text{instrument}} [定]_{\text{main verb}} [法度]_{\text{object}}]_{\text{predicate:VP}} \\
    sān-wáng jì yǐ {} dìng fǎ-dù \\
    three-king already using {} determine law-system \\
    \glt\translate{The three great kings had already established social institutions by [it].} \hfill {(荀子·大略)}
\end{exe}

The argument/adjunct distinction, or in the terminology of \citet{dixon2009basic}, the distinction between core and peripheral arguments, is however much less clear. 
Phenomena commonly attributed to the distinction (like obligatoriness) can often simply be explained by semantic and pragmatic reasons and/or idiomization.
Diagnostics based on structural closeness of the arguments to the main verb (e.g. pro-form substitution, extraction constraints) often fail to show qualitative distinctions.
Lexicalization of the argument structure, arguably the golden standard of the distinction,
is demonstrable in some but not all situations
\citep[\citepages{112-117, 125, 128, 212}]{mcinnerney2022argument}.

Lexicalization of verb frame is indeed attested in Classical Chinese (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.do-be-become}).
The structural closeness of arguments to the main verb is briefly discussed in \prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.prepositional}.

\subsubsection{The structure of the verb}
\label{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.word}

Classical Chinese completely lacks prototypical verb inflectional morphology,
rendering wordhood judgement difficult.

There exist monomorphemic forms typically heading clauses,
and hence due to this lexical specification of syntactic behaviors, 
these forms should be considered grammatical words. 

Whether multimorphemic forms should be considered words and hence instances of verb derivation is not clear,
because of the blurry boundary between words and larger units (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.morphology}).
As an instance, it is hard to determine if the second verb had already been incorporated into the first verb in the sequence 問曰 in a text when it was written.
We do not find any instance in Classical texts that require a verbal morphological analysis 
(問曰 for instance at least originally came from argument structure-level coordination; cf. \ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.complement-clause.direct-quotation.v-say.1}).

A peculiarity of Classical Chinese is that a root typically appearing in non-verbal environments can undergo ad hoc categorization and serve as the main verb (or more accurately, the \term{predicator} i.e. the core of the predicate).
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.word.noun-to-verb.1}) is a perfectly well-formed verbal clause:
it expresses a dynamic event and not just a state and has a modality auxiliary (illustrating that the predicate is not a noun phrase).
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.do-be-become.intransitive.1}) is a stative verbal clause involving ad hoc categorization.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.word.noun-to-verb.1}
    \glll
    [假 舟 楫 者]_{\text{subject}}， [非 能 水]_{\text{predicate:VP}} 也…… \\
    jiǎ zhōu jí zhě fēi néng shuǐ yě \\
    draw.help boat paddle \category{rel} \category{neg} can swim \category{sfp} \\
    \glt\translate{Those who draw help from boats and paddles cannot swim, \dots} \hfill (荀子·勸學)
\end{exe}

This ad hoc contextual categorization process differs from prototypical derivation in that it is irregular
(and thus it is not possible to generalize from the pattern in \ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.word.noun-to-verb.1} to a generic process).
Unlike the case in formal English,%
\footnote{
    Ad hoc categorization however is possible in informal English,
    as in \form{I might [guinea pig] it for you}
    and even the dephrasal \form{``Sappho and her friend'''d}.
}
in Classical Chinese ad hoc categorization of forms, which produces usages that are not lexically well established, is idiomatic:
the appearance of 水 in (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.word.noun-to-verb.1})
does not necessarily mean creation of a new verb \emph{lexical entry};
indeed its usage as a verb meaning \translate{to swim} is in fact rather rare.%
\footnote{
    In traditional terms, 詞類活用 \translate{word class flexibility} does not mean 一詞多義. 
}

In other clauses, the meaning of the root filling the main verb position of the clause can be regularly inferred,
and should thus be considered verbalizing zero derivation (or conversion, in the terminology of \citet[\citepages{1640-1641}]{cgel}).
An important process of this kind is the tropative (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.subject.topic-comparison.tropative.1}).



\subsubsection{Intransitive and transitive constructions}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.do-be-become}

The constituent order of transitive clauses is almost always SVO
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.do-be-become.interrogative.1}; but see \prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.high-level}).
Intransitive clauses have a SV constituent order
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.do-be-become.intransitive.1}).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.do-be-become.interrogative.1}
    \glll
    [子]_{\text{subject,A}} [[奚]_{\text{reason}} 不 [為]_{\text{verb},\category{do}} [政]_{\text{P}}]_{\text{predicate:VP}}？ \\
    zǐ xī bù wéi zhèng \\
    2 why \category{neg} conduct government \\
    \glt\translate{Why do you not take part in government?} \hfill{(論語·為政)}

    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.do-be-become.intransitive.1}
    \glll
    [君子]_{\text{subject,S}} [不 [器]_{\text{verb,\category{be}}}]_{\text{predicate:VP}} \\
    jūn-zǐ bù qì \\
    lord-son \category{neg} instrument \\
    \glt\translate{A gentleman does not resemble an instrument/container (i.e. his skills are broad).} \hfill{(論語·為政)}
\end{exe}

Consistent with cross-linguistically attested patterns, in Classical Chinese,
a monotransitive or intransitive clause 
can be about an intentionally initiated event (\category{do}; \ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.do-be-become.interrogative.1}, \prettyref{sec:valency.intransitive.action}),
a state (\category{be}; \ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.do-be-become.intransitive.1}, \prettyref{sec:valency.simple.state-and-change}) or a change of the state (\category{become}; \ref{ex:grammatical.clause.subject.topic-comparison.passive.1}, \prettyref{sec:valency.simple.state-and-change}).
The \category{do} type can further be divided into the transitive and intransitive classes.
\category{be} and \category{become} clauses are intransitive by definition.
Finally, \category{become} and \category{be} clauses are often inputs to the causative construction,
resulting in \category{cause}-\category{become}/\category{be} clauses.

The distinction between these constructions has long been noticed \citep[\citepages{92-96}]{li2004grammar}%
\footnote{
    More accurately, \citet{li2004grammar} classifies subtypes of objects (受動賓語 vs. 致動賓語).
    His classification maps exactly to the \category{do} vs. \category{cause}-\category{become} distinction.
    He also emphasizes what is essentially the difference between the S=A \category{do} labile valency and the S=P \category{become} labile valency.
}
and has consequences for
animacy and volition of the subject as well as the viability of argument structure alternation processes: S=A labile valency alternation naturally exists for \category{do} clauses (\prettyref{sec:valency.monotransitive.s-a}),
and S=P labile valency alternation naturally exists for \category{cause} clauses (\prettyref{sec:valency.monotransitive.s-p}).

Whether a verb is able to head a \category{do}, \category{be}, \category{become} or \category{cause} clause is  lexically specified
and is an important parameter in subclassifying verbs:
for instance, some causative verbs have no intransitive counterparts (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument-structure.causative.fossilization}) and eventually are reanalyzed as a \category{do} verb \citep[\citepages{269-271}]{meiguang2018},
and not all \category{do} verbs have S=A labile valency \citep[\citepage{93}]{li2004grammar}.

Some verbs also allow quantitative objects (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.tam.lexical.1}),
and are ``transitive'' superficially with a structure different from that of (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.do-be-become.interrogative.1}).

\subsubsection{Passive and causative constructions}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.valency-change}

Classical Chinese has a regular causative valency increasing construction producing \category{cause} clauses
(\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument-structure.causative}).
It typically is not compatible with a verb frame that already has an A-like argument \citep[\citepage{364}]{meiguang2018}.

There are also productive passive-like valency decreasing constructions, which suppress the subject 
and promote an internal argument to the subject position (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument-structure.passive}).
These constructions are different from prototypical passive \emph{voice} constructions
in that they are much more selective about the original verb frame.

(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.valency-change.1}) is an example of causativization on top of passive-like valency decreasing.
The verb 殺 is otherwise a \category{do} verb,
without the meaning of \translate{cause sb. to be killed}.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.valency-change.1}
    \glll
    …[殺]_{\text{main verb,\category{cause}-\category{``passive''}}} 御叔… \\
    shā Yùshū \\
    kill \category{name} \\
    \glt\translate{\dots caused Yushu to be killed.} 
    \hfill (左傳·成公二年)
\end{exe}

Classical Chinese seems to already have a prototype of what is later known as the disposal construction in later Sinitic languages (e.g. the 把 construction in Mandarin) (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.valency-change.disposal.1}).
Other verb phrase structure alternations with explicit grammatical markers include the 為…所 construction and more.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.valency-change.disposal.1}
    \glll
    盡 以 [其 寶器]_{\text{T}} 賂獻 [于 周 厘王]_{\text{G}} \\
    jìn yǐ qí bǎo-qì lù-xiàn yú Zhōu Xīwáng \\
    all \category{disposal} 3.\category{gen} precious-vessel bribe-offer to Zhou \category{name} \\
    \glt\translate{[They] offered all their precious vessels as gifts to King Xi of Zhou.} \hfill (史記·晉世家)
\end{exe}

\subsubsection{Prepositional arguments}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.prepositional}

(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.prepositional.give.1}, \ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.prepositional.ask.1}) are examples of prepositional constituents, carrying a wide range of semantic roles.
When an object is present, a rigid prepositional argument-after-object linear order can be observed after the main verb (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.prepositional.give.1}, \ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.prepositional.ask.1}).
Prepositional phrases (PPs) can also be fronted (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.high-level}).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.prepositional.give.1}
    \glll
    故 賜 [之]_{\text{G}} [以 重 祭]_{\text{T}} \\
    gù cì zhī yǐ zhòng jì \\
    therefore give 3 \category{prep} solemn offering \\
    \glt\translate{\dots and therefore gave him solemn venerations.} \hfill{(禮記·祭統)}

    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.prepositional.ask.1}
    \glll
    季康子 問 政 [于 孔子]_{\textbf{\text{addressee}}} \\
    Jì-Kāngzǐ wèn zhèng yú Kǒngzǐ \\
    \category{name} ask politics \category{prep} \category{name} \\
    \glt\translate{J. asks Confucius about politics.} \hfill{(論語·顏淵)}
\end{exe}

One issue surrounding licensing of prepositional arguments is whether some of them are licensed in so-called small clauses \citep{punske2013three}.
No convincing evidence for a small clause analysis is found in Classical Chinese;
clauses with structures comparable to (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.prepositional.ask.1}) cannot be analyzed in the small clause framework anyway \citep[\citepages{417-419}]{meiguang2018}.

Regardless of the validity of the small clause analysis, 
prepositional arguments stay in situ in every type of valency alternation, 
except for the applicative.
Many preposition arguments can be safely viewed as the least pivotal and the most internal argument
\citep[\citepage{404}]{meiguang2018},
though some, like those in passive-like constructions, are likely structurally more external \citep[\citepages{301-302}]{meiguang2018}. 

Some prepositional arguments can be replaced by non-prepositional objects (秦 in \ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.prepositional.no-prep.1}).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.prepositional.no-prep.1}
    \glll
    [入 [秦]_{\text{target}}]_{\text{temporal}} [何 見]_{\text{interrogative SOV VP}} \\
    rù Qín hé jiàn \\
    enter Qin what see \\
    \glt\translate{On entering Qin, what did you see?} \hfill{(荀子·強國)}
\end{exe}

\subsubsection{Applicative constructions}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.applicative}

Besides post-verbal preposition omission,
Classical Chinese has applicative constructions that
turn a clause containing a prepositional argument into a double object clause  
(\citealt[\citechap{10}]{meiguang2018}).

(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.applicative.ask.double-object.2}) illustrates an applicative clause where the target of the question becomes an object
(cf. \ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.prepositional.ask.1}).
The prepositional argument appears to be promoted to a more pivotal position
(\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.subject.argument-structure}),
as demonstrated by the linear order.


\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.applicative.ask.double-object.2}
    \glll
    上 問 [上林尉]_{\text{applicative addressee}} 諸 禽獸 簿 \\
    shàng wèn Shàng-lín-wèi zhū qín-shòu bù \\
    emperor ask upper-forest-administrator various bird-beast register \\
    \glt\translate{The emperor asked the superintendent of Shanglin about the registers of the various animals.} \hfill (史記·張釋之馮唐列傳)
\end{exe}

Other structurally comparable constructions include the tropative and the benefactive 
\citep[\citepages{389-415}]{meiguang2018}.
\citet[\citepage{420-421}]{meiguang2018} also notes that ditransitive clauses of giving and receiving in Classical Chinese may have a similar structure. 

Not all verb frames have applicative counterparts.
T-before-G applicative counterparts of (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.prepositional.give.1}) for instance are not attested.
Double objects may still appear by removing the preposition (cf. \ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.prepositional.no-prep.1}; \prettyref{sec:valency.ditransitive.locative.double-object}).

\subsubsection{Possession in argument structure}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.possession}

External possession is a heterogeneous phenomenon attested cross-linguistically \citep{deal2017external}.
Classical Chinese has a subject external possession construction,
apparently structurally parallel to Japanese external possession 
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.possession.1}). 

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.possession.1}
    \glll
    [子路]_{\text{subject,possessor}} [[性]_{\text{possessed}} [鄙]_{\text{predicate}}]_{\text{predicate}} \\
    Zǐlù xìng bǐ \\
    \category{name} nature uncultivated \\
    \glt\translate{Zilu was uncultivated by nature.} \hfill{(史記·仲尼弟子列傳)}
\end{exe}

(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.possession.1}) should not be analyzed as topicalization, because typically a pause cannot be inserted after 子路 (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.subject.topic-comparison}).
It should also not be analyzed as ordinary possession, as the sequence $X$性 meaning \translate{the nature of $X$} is rare if not impossible.

\subsubsection{Complement clause constructions}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.complement-clause}

Besides raising and control,
Classical Chinese has at least two complement clause constructions,
distinguishable by a structural complexity constraint:
it is not possible for the prototypical complement clause in (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.complement-clause.1}) to have sentence final particles;
on the other hand, the direct quotation (see \prettyref{sec:valency.direct-quotation}) in (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.complement-clause.direct-quotation.say.1}) is sentential and has a sentence final particle.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.complement-clause.1}
    \glll
    臣 竊 以為 [不 便 於 君]_{\text{\textbf{finite complement clause}}} \\
    chén qiè yǐwéi bù biàn yú jūn \\
    minister humbly consider \category{neg} advantageous to lord \\
    \glt\translate{I humbly consider [this] disadvantageous to you, my lord.} \hfill{(韓詩外傳·卷四)}

    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.complement-clause.direct-quotation.say.1}
    \glll
    子 曰： [“不 患 人 之 不 己 知， 患 不 知 人 也。”]_{\text{\textbf{direct quotation}}} \\
    zǐ yuē bù huàn rén zhī bù jǐ zhī huàn bù zhī rén yě \\
    master say \category{neg} worry person \category{gen} \category{neg} self know worry \category{neg} know person \category{sfp} \\
    \glt\translate{The Master said: ``Do not worry that others do not know you; worry that you do not know others.} \hfill{(論語·學而)}
\end{exe}

Whether the complement clause is a direct quotation is lexically determined.
In Classical Chinese, direct quotation is taken by a handful of verbs,
the most frequent one being 曰. 

Other verbs of speech, which themselves do not license complement clauses,
can introduce direct quotation by argument structure-level coordination
(\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.coordination}; \ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.complement-clause.direct-quotation.v-say.1}).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.complement-clause.direct-quotation.v-say.1}
    \glll
    [子禽]_{\text{subject: NP}} [[問]_{\text{verb}} [于 子貢]_{\text{target}}]_{\text{VP branch 1}} [[曰：“…”]_{\text{direct quotation}}]_{\text{VP branch 2}} \\
    Zǐqín wèn yú Zǐgòng yuē \\
    \category{name} ask at \category{name} say \\
    \glt\translate{Ziqin asked Zigong: \dots} \hfill{(論語·學而)}
\end{exe}


\subsubsection{Raising and control}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.raising-control}

It is possible for an argument of the complement clause (usually the A argument; \prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.subject.argument-structure}) to appear as a clausal dependent in the main clause.
Depending on when that argument is a part of the deep argument structure of the main clause or not,
such an argument-sharing construction can be classified as control or raising.
The underlying mechanisms of control constructions may again be heterogeneous,
a topic beyond the scope of this work.

Raising-to-object, object control, and similar argument sharing constructions are collectively known as 兼語式 or the \term{pivot construction} \citep[\citepage{40}]{pulleyblank1995outline}.
Uncontroversial pivot constructions are limited in number in early texts,
because many of them can also be analyzed as argument structure-level coordination
(\citealt[\citepage{376}]{meiguang2018}; \prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.coordination}).
Raising is rare in Classical Chinese \citep[\citepage{375-376}]{meiguang2018}.
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.raising-control.raising.1}) is an example of the pivot construction in Classical Chinese,
and is also an example of lexical causative (\prettyref{sec:valency.causative.analytic}),
analyzed as object raising in \citet[\citepage{375}]{meiguang2018} but may be alternatively analyzed as object control.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.raising-control.raising.1}
    \glll
    [令]_{\text{\textcolor{red}{main verb}}} [軍]_{\text{\textcolor{red}{P},\textcolor{blue}{A}}} [勿 敢 犯]_{\text{\textcolor{blue}{complement clause}}} \\
    lìng jūn wù gǎn fàn \\
    order army \category{neg} dare violate \\
    \glt\translate{[He] ordered/made the army not to dare violate [the prohibition].} \hfill{(韓非子·十過)}
\end{exe}


Subject control can also be observed in Classical Chinese (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.raising-control.control.2}).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.raising-control.control.2}
    \glll
    [吾]_{\text{subject,\textcolor{red}{A},\textcolor{blue}{A}}} 不 [欲]_{\text{\textcolor{red}{main verb}}} [觀 之]_{\text{\textcolor{blue}{complement clause}}} 矣 \\
    wú bù yù guān zhī yǐ \\
    1 \category{neg} want watch 3 \category{sfp} \\
    \glt\translate{I no longer wish to watch it.} \hfill{(論語·八佾)}
\end{exe}



\subsubsection{Verb phrase structure}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.high-level}

All arguments \citep[\citepages{3-5}]{he2005shiji}, include prepositional ones (\ref{ex:grammatical.np.preposition.omission.1}), can be omitted when they are identifiable from the context. 

Certain verb phrase constituents can appear before the main verb.
\ac{tam} adverbs almost appear before the main verb.
Below we discuss fronting of (core and peripheral) arguments.

First, the object pronoun is often regularly moved before the verb in a negative clause, sometimes hopping from a control clause into the main clause (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.high-level.sov.neg.1}), leading to a SOV order.
An interrogative object pronoun can also be fronted (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.prepositional.no-prep.1}).
This may be analyzed as the pronoun clitic being attached to the negator or the main verb and has diachronic variation \citep{aldridge2010cliticization}.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.high-level.sov.neg.1}
    \glll
    余  [不  女_i  忍  [殺 ---_i]]_{\text{predicate}} \\
    yú bù rǔ rěn shā \\
    1 \category{neg} 2 endure kill \\
    \glt\translate{I can't bear to kill you.} \hfill {(左傳·昭公元年)}
\end{exe}

Second, Classical Chinese appears to have a verb phrase internal focus position
(\citealt[\citepages{323-324}]{meiguang2018}; cf. \citealt{danckaert2011left}).

In (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.high-level.focus.1}),
the pre-verbal constituent 以其妹 seems to be a focalized argument.
It cannot be the sentential focus,
because the subject 季康子 seems to stay in-situ with no pause after it and therefore cannot be the sentential topic.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.high-level.focus.1}
    \glll
    季康子 [[以 其 妹]_{\text{VP-focus}_i} 妻 之 ---_{i}]_{\text{predicate:VP}} \\
    Jì-Kāngzǐ yǐ qí mèi qì zhī {} \\
    \category{name} with 3.\category{gen} younger.sister marry.to 3 {} \\
    \glt\translate{Ji Kangzi gave him his younger sister in marriage.} \hfill (左傳·哀公八年)
\end{exe}

The fronted prepositional phrase may further undergo preposition-object inversion (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.high-level.focus.2}).
This is much rarer than regular fronting of object pronouns,
consistent with the analysis that fronting in (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.high-level.focus.2})
has information structure values and is due to focalization.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.high-level.focus.2}
    \glll
    [[[室]_{\text{PP focus:NP_j}} 於 ---_j]_{\text{VP focus:PP_i}} 怒 ---_i]_{\text{VP}}， 市 於 色 \\
    shì yú {} nù {} shì yú sè \\
    home in {} be.angry {} market in show.expression \\
    \glt\translate{Being angry at home and showing it in the marketplace} \hfill (左傳·昭公十九年)
\end{exe}

\subsection{Tense, aspect, and modality}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.tam}

\emph{Syntactic} \ac{tam} categories (not just devices with semantic \ac{tam} meaning) are within a single clause
and form a system with a hierarchy of auxiliaries and adverbs.
Thus English \form{should have been doing} has grammaticalized \ac{tam} marking by auxiliaries, 
as the relative order of the auxiliaries cannot be shifted even with semantic motivations.
Adverbs like \form{now} or \form{frankly} follow a similar order constraint and are a part of the \ac{tam} system \citep{cinque1999adverbs}.%
\footnote{
    On the other hand, \form{be able to} is not a grammaticalized \ac{tam} marker (yet),
    as \form{many of us would have been able to have made this kind of bomb} is attested,
    where the \category{perfect} marking appears both before and after \form{able},
    modifying both the fact many of us being able to do something
    and the thing being done (i.e. making this kind of bomb),
    strongly suggesting that we are looking at a biclausal construction.
}

Because Classical Chinese have no native speakers, 
it is not possible to know if certain forms are impossible,
and therefore there is always room to argue that an alleged auxiliary verb is in fact a content verb
or certain unattested word orders are in fact possible.
Further, some temporal clausal modifiers may be circumstantial peripheral arguments and not \ac{tam} adverbs (\citealt[\citepages{29-30}]{cinque1999adverbs}; \citealt[\citepages{430-431}]{meiguang2018}).
The analyses below therefore are all tentative to a certain degree.




\subsubsection{Speaker-oriented categories}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.tam.speaker-oriented}

The most wide-scope \ac{tam}-like categories are usually categories related to the speaker's evaluation of the situation being talked about;
some of them can no longer be considered prototypically \ac{tam} categories.
Categories falling under this type include evaluative adverbs (like \form{frankly} at the start of a sentence), evidentiality, and epistemic modality.

Classical Chinese has a category somewhere between evidentiality and epistemic modality,
which may be known as speaker's commitment and has a subjective/objective distinction.
Strong speaker's commitment to objectivity of the state of affair being reported can be marked by the adverb 固 (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.tam.speaker-oriented.1}).
That the adverb 固 is always before the anteriority adverb 已 demonstrates its wide-scope, speaker-oriented nature.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.tam.speaker-oriented.1}
    \glll
    [固]_{\text{objectivity}} [已]_{\text{anteriority}} 明 矣 \\
    gù yǐ míng yǐ \\
    certainly already clear \category{sfp} \\
    \glt\translate{[This] certainly is already clear.} \hfill (春秋繁露·楚莊王)
\end{exe}

On the other hand, \citet[\citepage{359}]{li2004grammar} notes that clauses with certain sentence final particles,
which he calls 說明句, are more subjective.
The sentence initial particle 蓋 also marks an inferential attitude towards the situation being reported.

Some speaker-oriented adverbs, like 殆 in (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.tam.tense.1}),
may be interpreted as epistemic modality.

Classical Chinese has one category known as the \term{sentential aspect},
marked by a sentence final particle (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.tam.sentence-aspect.1}).
Its function is close to the sentence final 了 in Modern Standard Mandarin
(\citealt{paul2014particles}; \citealt{pan2021sentence}):
it is a wide-scope category indicating that the reported situation of affair is new
\citep[\citepage{116}]{pulleyblank1995outline},
and has been compared with inchoativity/evidentiality \citep{tsai2008tense}
and is comparable to the iamitive or the new situation marker in Southeastern Asian languages \citep{mazzitelli2025aspect}.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.tam.sentence-aspect.1}
    \glll
    此 迫 矣_{\text{sentential aspect}} \\
    cǐ pò yǐ \\
    this urgent \category{sfp} \\
    \glt\translate{This has become urgent.} \hfill (史記·項羽本紀)
\end{exe}

\subsubsection{Tense and modality}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.tam.tense}

Tense adverbs are attested in Classical Chinese. (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.tam.tense.1}) is an instance of adverbial marking of future.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.tam.tense.1}
    \glll
    夫子 [殆]_{\text{epistemic}} [將]_{\text{future}} 病 也 \\
    fū-zǐ dài jiāng bìng yě \\
    master-\category{hon} probably \category{fut} become.ill \category{sfp} \\
    \glt\translate{The Master will probably become ill.} \hfill (禮記·檀弓上)
\end{exe}

\citet[\citepage{428}]{meiguang2018} proposes that the sentence final particle 矣 introduces an explicit deictic time and therefore should be considered a (weak) tense marker.
\citet[\citepage{116}]{pulleyblank1995outline}, on the other hand, analyzes the particle as a ``new information'' sentential aspect.

Non-epistemic wide-scope modality categories about whether something has a chance to happen exist in Classical Chinese.
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.tam.ability.1}) illustrates a possibility adverb. 
A realis/irrealis distinction can be found in conditional constructions (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.linking.conditional.3}),
relevant to the licensing of \ac{tam} markers
\citep[\citepage{81}]{meiguang2018}.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.tam.ability.1}
    \glll
    我 [固]_{\text{objectivity}} [能]_{\text{possibility}} 治 偏枯 \\
    wǒ gù néng zhì piān-kū \\
    1 certainly can cure one.sided-paralysis \\
    \glt\translate{I certainly can cure paralysis on one side of the body.} \hfill (呂氏春秋·似順論·別類)
\end{exe}

\subsubsection{Aspect}

What is known as aspect includes anteriority i.e. secondary tense (English \form{have done}; \citealt[\citepage{140}]{cgel}), \citep{rizzi2016functional},
(im)perfectivity i.e. point of view aspect or progressive aspect,
the habitual aspect, and a lot more. 
These categories often have scope comparable to that of volition \citep{rizzi2016functional}.

We have already seen an instance of anteriority (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.tam.speaker-oriented.1}).
方 in (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.tam.progressive.1}) is a progressive adverb,
demonstrating existence of point of view aspect in Classical Chinese.
The adverb also has a prospective meaning.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.tam.progressive.1}
    \glll
    秦王 方 環 柱 走 \\
    Qín-wáng fāng huán zhù zǒu \\
    Qin-king \category{prog} circle pillar run \\
    \glt\translate{The king of Qin was running around a pillar.} \hfill (史記·刺客列傳)
\end{exe}

\subsubsection{Lexical aspect}

The lexical aspect is correlated to both argument structure and \ac{tam} categories
\citep{meisterernst2013verb,meisterernst2016syntax}.
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.tam.lexical.1}) illustrates how presence of a quantity argument turns the clause telic.
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.tam.lexical.2}) illustrates how the habitual aspect coerces an atelic reading.
It has also been observed that stative verbs are not compatible with perfective adverbs \citep{meisterernst2016syntax}.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.tam.lexical.1}
    \glll
    行 [二 百 餘 里]_{\text{quantity}} \\
    xíng èr bǎi yú lǐ \\
    travel two hundred more li \\
    \glt\translate{[They] travelled more than two hundred li.} \hfill (史記·衛將軍驃騎列傳)

    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.tam.lexical.2}
    \glll
    [常]_{\text{habitual}} 封 三 錢 之 府 \\
    cháng fēng sān qián zhī fǔ \\
    \category{hab} seal three currency \category{gen} treasury \\
    \glt\translate{[He] regularly sealed the treasury of the three currencies.} \hfill (史記·越王句踐世家)
\end{exe}

\subsection{Subjecthood}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.subject}

In \prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.nominal} and in \prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal},
we both mention the concept of \term{subject}.
This concept of a single, unitary pivot in a clause should not be taken for granted.

Two types of \term{pivots} exist in a clause:
one based on argument structure properties like obligatory argument omission in control constructions
or binding of reflexive pronouns
(\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.subject.argument-structure}),
another based on clausal pivot properties like subject sharing in coordination and relativization
(\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.subject.clause-pivot}).
Prototypical subjecthood is identification of the two pivots
and hence absence of syntactic ergativity \citep{aldridge2008generative},
discussed in the sections below.

Another problem is the distinction between subject and topic,
both appearing at the beginning of a clause with unclear structural differences.
Some scholars treat any seemingly pivotal constituent a subject
(e.g. \citealt[\citepage{41}]{li2004grammar})
and hence the topic is a ``big subject''
\citep[\citepage{42}]{li2004grammar}.
This opinion appears to suggest that Classical Chinese has only information structure and no argument structure in its syntax
\citep[\citepage{122}]{meiguang2018}.
We discuss the issue in \prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.subject.topic-comparison}.

\subsubsection{Subject vs. topic}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.subject.topic-comparison}

We argue that identifying subject and topic (as in \citealt[\citepages{41-42}]{li2004grammar}) is not desirable for describing Classical Chinese.

First, in reading traditions of Classical Chinese (as indicated by traditional 句讀 punctuations), some pivotal constituents are followed by a pause,
while others are obligatorily not followed by a pause \citep[\citepages{121-122}]{meiguang2018}. 
No existing theory equating subject and topic naturally explains the distinction;
on the other hand, availability of the pause is a cross-linguistic indication of topicalization.

Second, the constituent closest to the main verb often has an obligatorily semantic role.

In tropative clauses, the pivotal constituent closest to the main verb 
obligatorily refers to the person holding the opinion.
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.subject.topic-comparison.tropative.1}),
where the main verb is obviously a tropative use of the noun 客 \translate{guest},
can only be interpreted as \translate{Lord Mengchang considers me as a guest}
(and never \translate{I consider Lord Mengchang as a guest}).
This implies that the argument structure of the clause imposes a hard constraint on 
what argument can be promoted to a pivotal constituent,
which is unusual for topicalization.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.subject.topic-comparison.tropative.1}
    \glll
    孟嘗君 [客]_{\text{main verb,tropative}} 我 \\
    Mèngcháng-jūn kè wǒ \\
    \category{name}-lord guest 1 \\
    \glt\translate{Lord Mengchang treats me as a guest.} \hfill (戰國策·齊人有馮諼者)
\end{exe}

When the pre-verbal pivotal constituent closest to the main verb is semantically not the agent,
it is often \emph{obligatorily} so according to the lexical properties of the verb (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.subject.topic-comparison.passive.1}),
which again is unusual for topicalization
(but not unusual if interpreted as valency alternation).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.subject.topic-comparison.passive.1}
    \glll
    [國 [定]_{\text{main verb,\category{become}}}]_{\text{clause branch 1}} 而 [天下 定]_{\text{clause branch 2}} \\
    guó dìng ér tiān-xià dìng \\
    state become.settled \category{conj} heaven-under become.settled \\
    \glt\translate{The state becomes settled, and all under heaven becomes settled.} \hfill (荀子·王霸) \\
    \translate{*The state settles someone,\dots}
\end{exe}

Therefore, pivotal constituents that are not topics and are structurally closer to the main verb than prototypical topics exist in Classical Chinese.

\subsubsection{Pivot in argument structure}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.subject.argument-structure}

Cross-linguistically, the pivot at the level of argument structure, distinct from the clausal pivot, is often the A-like argument in a clause even in syntactically ergative languages.
In Classical Chinese this can be demonstrated by examining the shared argument in control constructions (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.raising-control}).
The reflexive pronoun binding test is not available in Classical Chinese,
as reflexive-like forms may have logophoric or emphatic distributions.

Further, the existence of an argument structure pivot can be confirmed by observing valency alternation constructions,
as most of them involve either promotion of an argument to an A-like position 
or removal of the A-like argument.

It is sometimes also possible to define the second most pivotal argument.
In applicative clauses, the promoted argument appears in the pre-verbal position,
which can be interpreted as it being promoted to a structurally more pivotal position
(\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.applicative}, \ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.applicative.ask.double-object.2}).
In double object \translate{giving} clauses,
it is usually the G argument being promoted to the subject position in valency decreasing (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.subject.argument-structure.giving.1}),
which again can be interpreted as the G argument being structurally more pivotal.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.subject.argument-structure.giving.1}
    \glll
    [諸侯]_{\text{G}}， 賜 [弓矢]_{\text{T}} 然後 征 \\
    zhū-hóu cì gōng-shǐ rán-hòu zhēng \\
    various-lord grant bow-arrow then campaign \\
    \glt\translate{The feudal lords are granted bows and arrows and only then undertake military campaigns.} \hfill (禮記·王制)
\end{exe}


Hierarchies of pivotality or ``externality'' of arguments are not uncommon in world languages.%
\footnote{
    In Japhug, a language with direct-inverse alignment,
    it is possible to have a clause containing a causer, an instrument, an agent and a patient. 
    The personal indexation marker appears to be decided on the basis of the hierarchy
    \term{causer}\textto\term{instrument}\textto\term{agent}\textto\term{patient},
    by first taking the two most internal arguments and deciding which is more salient on the empathy hierarchy,
    then comparing the result with the third most internal argument and deciding which is more salient,
    and finally comparing the result of the last step with the most external argument;
    hence 1\textto 3\textto 2\textto 3 is morphologically equivalent to 1\textto 2
    \citep[\citepage{310};\citepage{584},(116);\citepage{848},(67)]{jacques2021grammar}. 
    Similar mechanisms are attested cross-linguistically \citet[\citesec{7.4.1}]{wiltschko2014universal}.
}

\subsubsection{Clausal pivot}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.subject.clause-pivot}

We then argue that the A-like, most pivotal argument in a verbal clause 
and the initial constituent in a nominal clause 
are clause pivots, around which everything else ``revolves''.

The clearest criterion that defines clausal pivotal subjecthood is probably coordination:
when clauses are coordinated,
one constituent seems to be shared by all of them,
then this constituent is probably the clausal pivot,
which is usually the argument structure pivot in nominative-accusative languages.

In Classical Chinese, clauses with 而 coordination overwhelmingly have S=A coordination.
This is illustrated in (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.coordination.subject-vp.1}),
in which the two coordinated clauses share the argument structure pivot, meaning that the S and A arguments are in comparable clausal grammatical functions.

S=P coordination, on the other hand, can be attributed to either topicalization or passivization
(e.g. \prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.coordination}, \ref{ex:grammatical.clause.coordination.topic.1}).
Therefore, Classical Chinese clauses appear to uniformly promote the most pivotal argument to the clausal pivot position,
consistent with the nominative-accusative alignment.

\subsection{Information packaging}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.information}

\subsubsection{Topicalization}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.topic}

Topicalization is ubiquitous in Classical Chinese.
Indeed, the vanilla nominal predication construction (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.nominal.isa.1}) 
is much less frequent than the so-called 判斷句 \translate{judgmental clause},
in which the subject is topicalized, often with the sentence final particle 也 (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.topic.nominal.1}),
or the topic particle 者 (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.topic.nominal.2}), or both.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.topic.nominal.1}
    \glll
    [城 北 徐-公]_{\text{topic:\ac{np}}}， [齊-國 之 美-麗 者]_{\text{comment}} 也 \\
    chéng běi Xú-gōng Qí-guó zhī měi-lì zhě yě \\
    city north \category{name}-\category{gong} Qi-country \category{gen} beautiful-beautiful \category{rel} \category{sfp} \\
    \glt\translate{Mr. Xu from the north of the city is a handsome man in the country Qi.} \hfill (戰國策·鄒忌脩八尺有餘)

    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.topic.nominal.2}
    \glll
    [兵]_{\text{topic: \ac{np}}} 者， [不 祥 之 器]_{\text{comment, predicate}} \\
    bīng zhě bù xiáng zhī qì \\
    weapon \category{topic} \category{neg} fortunate \category{gen} instrument \\
    \glt\translate{Weapons are not auspicious.} \hfill (老子)
\end{exe}

Topicalization, and the topic marker 者, is not restricted to the nominal predication construction
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.topic.verbal.1}).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.topic.verbal.1}
    \glll
    此 二 人 者， 實 弒 寡君 \\
    cǐ èr rén zhě shí shì guǎ-jūn \\
    this two person \category{topic} indeed murder.ruler our-lord \\
    \glt\translate{These two men indeed murdered our lord.} \hfill (左傳·隱公四年)
\end{exe}

What is topicalized is of course not restricted to the subject.
This fact is a piece of evidence supporting the distinction between subject and topic in Classical Chinese.
In (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.topic.predicate.1}),
the comment clearly has a nominal predicate.
What is promoted to the topic position however is not the subject of the nominal predicate construction,
but the predicate. The subject is likely the \emph{focus} and not the topic
\citep[\citepage{138}]{meiguang2018}.
Topicalization of other clausal constituents is also possible
(e.g. \prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.coordination}, \ref{ex:grammatical.clause.coordination.topic.1}).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.topic.predicate.1}
    \glll
    [仁 之 實]_{\text{topic:NP_i}}， [[事 親]_{\text{subject}} [是]_{\text{predicate_i}}]_{\text{comment}} 也 \\
    rén zhī shí shì qīn shì yě \\
    benevolence \category{gen} substance serve parent this \category{sfp} \\
    \glt\translate{As for the substance of benevolence, serving your parents is it.} \hfill (孟子·離婁上)
\end{exe}


Some topic-like constituents, mainly prepositional phrases, are analyzed as \term{frames} in \citet[\citepages{129,323}]{meiguang2018}.


\subsubsection{Focalization}

Topicalization is marked by fronting, 
but what is fronted is not necessarily a topic.
In (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.focus.vp-fronted.1}),
for instance, the verbal predicate is fronted,
which likely is not topical.
Note that the sentence final particle is fronted as well.


\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.focus.vp-fronted.1}
    \glll
    [[甚]_{\text{VP_i}} 矣]_{\text{focus}}， [[汝 之 不 惠]_{\text{subject:clause (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.force.nonfinite})}} ---_{\text{predicate_i}}] \\
    shèn yǐ rǔ zhī bù huì {} \\
    extreme \category{sfp} 2 \category{gen} \category{neg} smart  {} \\
    \glt\translate{You are so stupid! (lit. So extreme is your being unintelligent!)} \hfill (列子·湯問)
\end{exe}

\subsection{Speech acts and clause types}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.force}

This section discusses high-level grammatical categories which, in the case of sentential clauses, are often related to the speech act of the clause.
These categories are traditionally known as \term{clause types} or 句類, as opposed to 句型 i.e. the internal structure of clauses \citep[\citepage{58}]{li2004grammar}.

\subsubsection{Sentential clauses: interrogative, exclamative, and imperative}

The speech force of sentential clauses are marked by sentence final particles.
In fact, these particles almost only appear in sentential clauses.

Interrogative clauses may completely lack formal marking except interrogative pro-forms (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.prepositional.no-prep.1}).
The interrogative speech force is optionally marked by 乎 and other particles
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.force.interrogative.1}).
The exclamative speech act is similarly marked by sentence final particles
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.force.exclamative.1}).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.force.interrogative.1}
    \glll
    [其 能 久]_{\text{nucleus}} 乎_{\text{speech force}}？ \\
    qí néng jiǔ hū \\
    \category{modal} can last.long \category{q} \\
    \glt\translate{Can [this] last long?} \hfill (史記·周本紀)

    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.force.exclamative.1}
    \glll
    [吾 死]_{\text{nucleus}} 矣_{\text{sentential aspect}} 夫_{\text{speech force}}! \\
    wú sǐ yǐ fú \\
    1 die \category{sfp} \category{excl} \\
    \glt\translate{I am going to die!} \hfill (孟子·離婁下)
\end{exe}

We note that in (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.force.exclamative.1}),
the sentential aspect marker 矣 (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.tam.speaker-oriented}) appears before the exclamative 夫,
which means that the two systems of particles can coexist.
The reverse order *夫矣 is not attested.

\subsubsection{Nonfinite constructions}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.force.nonfinite}

Classical Chinese has one clausal construction that is structurally clearly different from sentential clauses, and may be known as nonfinite.

It is possible to insert 之 between the subject and the predicate of a clause.
The result appear as the object
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.force.nonfinite.1})
or as a conditional or temporal clause
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.force.nonfinite.2}; \prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.linking}).
Sentence final particles are never attested in this type of clauses.

This process is sometimes known as nominalization \citep[\citepage{64}]{pulleyblank1995outline}.
This term cannot be taken literally in all situations:
in (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.force.nonfinite.2}), the ``nominalized'' clause does not admit adjectival modification
and takes an object, both unusual for a noun phrase.
The example therefore has a structure comparable to the English gerundive non-finite clause \form{his playing the national anthem}
and is structurally different from the noun phrase.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.force.nonfinite.1}
    \glll
    王 如 知 此， 則 無 望 [民 之 多 于 鄰 國]_{\text{object:\form{zhī}-construction}} 也 \\
    wáng rú zhī cǐ zé wú wàng mín zhī duō yú lín guó yě \\
    king if know this then \category{neg} hope people \category{gen} more than neighbor country \category{sfp} \\
    \glt\translate{If Your Majesty knows this, then don't expect your people to be more plentiful than your neighboring countries' people.} \hfill (孟子·梁惠王上)

    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.force.nonfinite.2}
    \glll
    [父母 之 愛 子]_{\text{condition:\form{zhī}-construction}}， 則 為 之 計 深遠 \\
    fù-mǔ zhī ài zǐ zé wèi zhī jì shēn-yuǎn \\
    father-mother \category{gen} love child then for 3 plan deep-far \\
    \glt\translate{When parents love their children, they plan far ahead for them.} \hfill (戰國策·趙太后新用事)
\end{exe}

\subsection{Subordination}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.linking}

The term \term{subordination} sometimes means all kinds of clause embedding.
In this section we primarily focus on bipartite clauses
with the structure and meaning of \translate{if \dots then \dots} or \translate{when ...}.
Complement clauses are discussed in \prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.complement-clause} and \prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.raising-control}.
Relative clauses are discussed in \prettyref{sec:grammatical.np.relative}.

An overview of subordination constructions in Classical Chinese can be found in \citet[\citechap{3}]{meiguang2018}.
In all Classical Chinese conditional constructions,
the condition usually appears before the consequence
(\prettyref{ex:grammatical.clause.linking.conditional.1},
\prettyref{ex:grammatical.clause.linking.conditional.3}).
The consequence can be marked by a particle (e.g. 則; \prettyref{ex:grammatical.clause.linking.conditional.1}).

The condition clause can also be marked but zero marking is possible.
Classical Chinese distinguishes between realis and irrealis conditional constructions
(\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.tam.tense}):
the former are marked by e.g. 既 (\prettyref{ex:grammatical.clause.linking.conditional.1}),
while the latter are marked by e.g. 若 (\prettyref{ex:grammatical.clause.linking.conditional.3}).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.linking.conditional.1}
    \glll
    [既 來 之]_{\text{condition}}， [則 安 之]_{\text{consequence}} \\
    jì lái zhī zé ān zhī \\
    already cause.to.come 3 then settle 3 \\
    \glt\translate{Once you have brought them here, settle them.} \hfill (論語·季氏)

    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.linking.conditional.3}
    \glll
    [若 已 食] 則 退 \\
    ruò yǐ shí zé tuì \\
    if already eat then withdraw \\
    \glt\translate{If [the parent] has already eaten, then withdraw.} \hfill (禮記·內則)
\end{exe}


An interesting phenomenon is that the subordinated clause can be a \form{zhī}-construction clause (\prettyref{ex:grammatical.clause.force.nonfinite.2}).
This is cross-linguistically unsurprising (as in Japanese or Turkish).
Note that the marker 若 can be attached to the gerundive condition clause as well
\citep[\citepage{98}]{meiguang2018}.
Other interesting features of the conditional clause include that 
the condition marker can sometimes appear after the subject.

Besides clausal-level subordination, Classical Chinese also likely has verb phrase-level subordination.
In (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.linking.vp.1}),
the first verb phrase (whose agent is \emph{obligatorily} the subject of the clause) describes the manner of the second,
and thus should be understood as subordination.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.linking.vp.1}
    \glll
    吾 嘗 [跂]_{\text{modifier verb phrase}} 而 [望]_{\text{main verb phrase}} 矣 \\
    wú cháng qì ér wàng yǐ \\
    1 once stand.on.tiptoe \category{conj} gaze \category{sfp} \\
    \glt\translate{I once stood on tiptoe and gazed into the distance.} \hfill (荀子·勸學)
\end{exe}

\subsection{Coordination}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.coordination}

Coordination happens at the level of clause, (extended) verb phrase or argument structure.
The distinction between the last two is that 
coordination of two argument structures results in a \emph{single} situation of affairs,
usually with shared \ac{tam} marking,
while coordination of two verb phrases is more comparable to clausal coordination \citep[\citepages{192-201}]{meiguang2018}.
The two also have differences in relativization \citep[\citepage{207}]{meiguang2018}.


In Classical Chinese, certain examples are clearly argument structure-level coordination (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.complement-clause.direct-quotation.v-say.1}).
Others are clearly not, including those in which the two verb phrase branches are separated by a pause \citep[\citepage{195}]{meiguang2018}.
However, due to the absence of obligatory \ac{tam} marking (and unavailability of acceptability judgement data), marginal cases in the classification above exist in Classical Chinese.
See (TODO) for a detailed analysis.

Coordination is primarily marked by 而 (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.coordination.subject-vp.1}),
which is not used in noun phrase coordination.
Note that the functions of 而 are not restricted to conjunction (\citealt[\citepage{183}]{meiguang2018}; \ref{ex:grammatical.clause.linking.vp.1}).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.coordination.subject-vp.1}
    \glll
    項王 [按 劍]_{\text{VP branch 1}} 而 [跽]_{\text{VP branch 2}} \\
    Xiàng-wáng àn jiàn ér jì \\
    \category{name}-king hold sword \category{conj} rise.on.knees \\
    \glt\translate{King Xiang held his sword and raised himself on his knees.} \hfill (史記·項羽本紀)
\end{exe}



\subsubsection{Topic chains as syntactic coordination}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.coordination.topic-chain}

\begin{todobox}{}{topic-chain}
    Where to put this section? And what's the actual relation between topicalization and coordination?
\end{todobox}

An interesting question is the interaction between topicalization and coordination.
\citet[\citepage{217}]{meiguang2018} contends that ``topic chains'',
i.e. several clauses with a shared topic \citep[\citechap{4} \citesec{3.3}]{meiguang2018},
are discourse structures and not syntactic structures.
Therefore topicalization happens first, and coordination happens then:
after that no further topicalization is possible.
He further argues that clauses in a topic chain cannot be linked together by 而.
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.coordination.topic.1}) however seems to be a counterexample.
This example clearly contains two coordinated clauses.
In the first clause 取之于藍, 之, appearing after the verb, 
can only be a pronoun, and the only sensible reading of the clause
is that 之 (the object) is coreferential with 青 at the initial of the sentence,
and 取之于藍 then means \translate{(people) extract it (i.e. indigo dye) from \species{Indigofera}.}
Therefore, 青 at the initial of the sentence is the object of the first clause
and the subject of the second clause,
meaning it cannot be the shared subject.
This, together with the traditional pause after the first 青,
means the first 青 likely is a topic,
which means here topicalization happens \emph{after} coordination.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.coordination.topic.1}
    \glll
    [青]_{\text{topic: NP_i}}， ---_i 取 [之]_{\text{object: Pronoun_i}} 于 藍 而 ---_i 青 于 藍 \\
    qīng {} qǔ zhī yú lán ér {} qīng yú lán \\
    indigo.dye {} pick it from \species{Indigofera} \category{conj} {} blue than \species{Indigofera} \\
    \glt\translate{Indigo dye, people extract it from \species{Indigofera}, but it's bluer than \species{Indigofera}.} \hfill (荀子·勸學)
\end{exe}

\section{The noun phrase}\label{sec:grammatical.np}

The Classical Chinese \ac{np} can be roughly divided into 
the determiner region and the ``core'' region,
the latter known in \citet{cgel} as the \term{nominal}.%
\footnote{
    In this note, when the term \term{nominal} is used as a noun,
    it refers to the determined region in \ac{np}s,
    while when it is used as an adjective,
    it refers to the status of being the head of a \ac{np}. 
}
The latter is the head noun plus possible arguments and modifiers,
and the former can be left empty or filled by a demonstrative or a ``possessor'',
the role of the latter being not confined to a semantic possessor
(\citealt[\citepage{61}]{pulleyblank1995outline}; \prettyref{sec:grammatical.np.determiner}).

\subsection{Structure of the noun}\label{sec:grammatical.np.word}

\subsubsection{Wordhood}\label{sec:grammatical.np.word.wordhood}

Like the case of the verb (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.word}),
it is hard to demarcate the boundary of the Classical Chinese noun with absolute certainty
based solely on evidence in the texts.

The golden standard of morphological templates (which are not completely syntactically motivated) does not apply to Classical Chinese nouns,
as it has no inflectional or template derivational morphology.

Some monomorphemic forms, like 客, typically head noun phrases and when they do head clauses,
they have meanings regularly inferrable from their nominal uses (as in the tropative example \ref{ex:grammatical.clause.subject.topic-comparison.tropative.1}).
They thus should be regarded as grammatical words, due to lexical specification of their grammatical behaviors.

This however does not say anything about wordhood of composite forms like (\ref{ex:grammatical.np.word.1}).
Classical Chinese has much more composite nominal forms with stable, established, non-inferrable meanings.
This is however not decisive as it can be explained by idiomization.

Composite nominal forms are often built by processes with complexity constraints,
which some take as evidence for wordhood.
In (\ref{ex:grammatical.np.word.1}), the modifier usually cannot be modified by adjectives.
Structural complexity constraints nonetheless appear in structures that are clearly not words (cf. \prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.complement-clause}).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.np.word.1}
    \glll
    [舟]_{\text{modifier}}[人] \\
    zhōu-rén \\
    boat-person \\
    \glt\translate{boatman} \hfill (韓詩外傳·卷四)
\end{exe}

A composite nominal form obligatorily heading a noun phrase does not need to be explained by lexical specification of the composite form as a whole:
it may be simply explained by the head of the form being from a noun lexical entry.
To use the lexical specification criterion of wordhood we have to provide something structure, 
like unpredictable specification of the argument structure (cf. the discussion in \prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal}).
This is not easy, either for monomorphemic or composite nominal forms. 
Because there is no obligatory possession in Classical Chinese, the argument structure lexical specification argument has to rely on very subtle evidence (see \ref{ex:grammatical.np.argument.no-poss.1}).%
\footnote{
    Pre-Classical Old Chinese might have traits of inalienable possession \citep[\citepage{57}]{baxter2014old} (which is speculative; \citealt[\citepage{141}]{hill2019historical}) but this did not automatically transmit to the Classical period.
}

On the other hand, there is clear semantic lexicalization: (\ref{ex:grammatical.np.word.2}) obligatorily means \translate{a minister serving the sovereign}, never, say, \translate{a minister who happens to reside in the sovereign}.
Whether this originates from grammatical lexical argument structure  specification is not undebatable.%
\footnote{
    Meanwhile, at least some verbal lexical entries syntactically select their arguments,
    as in \prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.complement-clause}.
    A similar diagnostic is available for English nouns \citep[\citepage{440}]{cgel}
    but not for Classical Chinese, 
    as noun phrase arguments in Classical Chinese typically lack any marking of their grammatical types.
}


\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.np.word.2}
    \glll
    社稷 之 臣 \\
    shè-jì zhī chén \\
    altar.of.soil-altar.of.grain \category{gen} minister \\
    \glt\translate{a minister serving the state} \hfill (論語·季氏)
\end{exe}

In summary, similar to the case of verbs,
noun wordhood cannot be universally established for multimorphemic nominal forms in Classical Chinese.
The definition of wordhood of composite nominal forms in this work 
therefore has to rely on indirect evidence and inference of best explanation:
cross-linguistically, there are much more words than phrasal idioms,
and parts in a form like (\ref{ex:grammatical.np.word.1}) are often though not always aggregated into a word,
and thus convergence of these imperfect wordhood criteria indicates wordhood.

\subsubsection{Nominalization}

Multiple constructions have been known as nominalization.
The 之-insertion (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.force.nonfinite}) is sometimes considered nominalization.
Interestingly, relativization in Classical Chinese may indeed be nominalization (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.np.relative}).

Classical Chinese also has nominalization constructions targeting structurally simpler forms.
In (\ref{ex:grammatical.np.nominalization.1}),
堅 refers to \translate{what are hard (i.e. defensive gears)}
and 銳 refers to \translate{offensive weapons},
semantically comparable to relative clauses and analyzable as zero derivation (cf. discussion on derivation in \prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.word}).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.np.nominalization.1}
    \glll
    被 堅 執 銳 \\
    pī jiān zhí ruì \\
    wear hard hold sharp \\
    \glt\translate{wear armour and wield weapons} \hfill (史記·陳涉世家)
\end{exe}

\subsection{Argument structure}

Classical Chinese noun phrases have rather limited positions for arguments:
there is no prepositional argument comparable to English \form{normalization [of this behavior]}.
Typically there is only one dependent that can be reasonably considered an argument.
It often appears in the possessor position (\ref{ex:grammatical.np.word.2}),
but not always (\ref{ex:grammatical.np.argument.no-poss.1}).
In fact, the possessive 之 is never inserted into (\ref{ex:grammatical.np.argument.no-poss.1}).
This appears to be a lexically specified behavior and indicates existence of core/peripheral argument distinction.



\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.np.argument.no-poss.1}
    \glll
    [櫟陽]_{\text{location}} 獄掾 \\
    Yuèyáng yù-yuàn \\
    Yueyang prison-clerk \\
    \glt\translate{a prison clerk in Yueyang} \hfill (史記·項羽本紀)
\end{exe}

We also note the meaning difference between (\ref{ex:grammatical.np.word.2}) and (\ref{ex:grammatical.np.argument.no-poss.2}),
which suggests that there exists more than one argument-like position in the noun phrase.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.np.argument.no-poss.2}
    \glll
    社稷 臣 \\
    shè-jì chén \\
    altar.of.soil-altar.of.grain  minister \\
    \glt\translate{a minister important for the state (lit. a state-level minister)} \hfill (史記·袁盎鼂錯列傳)
\end{exe}



\subsection{Adjectives}

Cross-linguistically, what are known as adjectives can be divided into inner direct modifications and outer (reduced) relative clauses \citep[\citepage{23-25}]{cinque2010syntax}.

Direct modification exists in Classical Chinese,
in which adjectives tend to have a relatively fixed order depending on what properties they describe
(\ref{ex:grammatical.np.direct-modification.1}; cf. English \form{a big gray bear}).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.np.direct-modification.1}
    \glll
    大 白 狗 \\
    dà bái gǒu \\
    big white dog \\
    \glt\translate{a big white dog} \hfill (漢書·五行志)
\end{exe}

It should be noted that examples involving multiple pre-nominal attributives are scarce.
Complex attributives are much more idiomatically constructed using the relative clause construction (\ref{ex:grammatical.np.relative.long.1}).

\subsection{Relative clause constructions}
\label{sec:grammatical.np.relative}

Classical Chinese has a fused relative clause construction marked by 者.
A fused relative clause can take a determiner
(\ref{ex:grammatical.np.relative.modify.1}),
and hence may be analyzed as a nominal \citep{aldridge2009old}.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.np.relative.modify.1}
    \glll
    晉 之 從政 者 \\
    Jìn zhī cóng-zhèng zhě \\
    Jin \category{gen} engage.in-government \category{rel} \\
    \glt\translate{those who take part in government in Jin} \hfill (左傳·宣公十二年)
\end{exe}

(\ref{ex:grammatical.np.relative.long.1}) is an instance of a long, complex relative clause in Classical Chinese.
This illustrates that we are indeed dealing with a relative clause construction 
and not a low-level nominalization construction.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.np.relative.long.1}
    \glll
    若 [--- 至 力 農 畜， --- 工 虞 商 賈， --- 為 權利 以 成 富， [--- 大 者 傾 郡]， [--- 中 者 傾 縣]， [--- 下 者 傾 鄉里]] 者， 不 可 勝 數 \\
    ruò {} zhì lì nóng xù {} gōng yú shāng gǔ {} wéi quán-lì yǐ chéng fù {} dà zhě qīng jùn {} zhōng zhě qīng xiàn {} xià zhě qīng xiāng-lǐ zhě bù kě shèng shǔ \\
    as.for {} utmost exert.oneself farm rear.livestock {} work.as.artisan manage.natural.resources trade trade {} pursue power-profit to achieve wealth {} great \category{rel} surpass commandery {} middle \category{rel} surpass county {} low \category{rel} surpass village-neighbourhood \category{rel} \category{neg} can exhaust count \\
    \glt\translate{As for those who devote their efforts to farming and livestock, crafts, natural resources or trade, or pursue power and profit to become rich---the greatest surpassing commanderies, the middling counties, and the least villages and neighbourhoods---they cannot all be counted.} \hfill (史記·貨殖列傳)
\end{exe}

When the 者 relative clause is used as a non-fused relative clause,
it is always postpositive.
The structural analysis of the construction is discussed in TODO.

Classical Chinese has pre-nominal relative clauses too (\ref{ex:grammatical.np.relative.pre.1}).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.np.relative.pre.1}
    \glll
    得 志 之 君子 \\
    dé zhì zhī jūn-zǐ \\
    attain aspiration \category{rel} lord-son \\
    \glt\translate{a gentleman who has attained his aspirations} \hfill (春秋繁露·竹林)
\end{exe}

\subsection{The determiner system}\label{sec:grammatical.np.determiner}

A demonstrative-before-numeral order can be observed in the determiner system
(\ref{ex:grammatical.np.determiner.dem.1}).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.np.determiner.dem.1}
    \glll
    此 三 姦 者 \\
    cǐ sān jiān zhě \\
    this three wicked \category{rel} \\
    \glt\translate{these three wicked men} \hfill (荀子·非十二子)
\end{exe}

The ``possessor'' (which does not necessarily have possessive meaning), when present, is separated by the particle 之 from the nominal region
(\ref{ex:grammatical.np.determiner.gen.1}).
It appears that the possessor, the demonstrative, and the pre-nominal relative clause (\ref{ex:grammatical.np.relative.pre.1}) are in competition 
(while they coexist with the numeral).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.np.determiner.gen.1}
    \glll
    君 之 三 臣 \\
    jūn zhī sān chén \\
    lord \category{gen} three minister \\
    \glt\translate{your three ministers} \hfill (禮記·檀弓下)
\end{exe}

\subsection{Prepositions}

What fall under the name of preposition include 
those that are roughly non-morphological grammatical relation markers,
those from directional constructions,
and transitive adverbs.

Classical Chinese does not have modern Sinitic directional constructions of the shape of 在……上,
and appears to have no transitive adverbs.
Prepositions are restricted to markers of oblique clausal dependents.

Prepositions in Classical Chinese often have diachronic verbal etymology,
but at least some of them have already fully grammaticalized.
The meaning of 於 in (\ref{ex:grammatical.np.preposition.1}) cannot be reasonably analyzed as verbal.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.np.preposition.1}
    \glll
    且 貳 於 楚 也 \\
    qiě èr yú Chǔ yě \\
    and have.divided.allegiance to Chu \category{sfp} \\
    \glt\translate{[Zheng] also owed allegiance to Chu.} \hfill (左傳·僖公三十年)
\end{exe}

It is possible to omit the complement of a preposition.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.np.preposition.omission.1}
    \glll
    愚 不 足 與 謀 智 \\
    yú bù zú yǔ móu zhì \\
    foolish \category{neg} sufficient with discuss wisdom \\
    \glt\translate{The foolish are not worth discussing wisdom with.} \hfill (荀子·正論)
\end{exe}

\section{Parts of speech division}\label{sec:grammatical.pos}


Frequent zero derivation and ad hoc categorization of words and the lack of inflectional endings in Classical Chinese have led some to argue that the language lacks parts of speech division.%
\footnote{
    If a noun is defined as the head of a noun phrase, then trivially Classical Chinese has a noun/verb distinction.
    Here we talk about form classes in the lexicon, and not the grammatical functions of the forms.
    See also the beginning of this chapter.
}

This statement cannot be completely correct: 
in \prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.word} and \prettyref{sec:grammatical.np.word.wordhood}
we have demonstrated that content morphemes have clear preferences for nominal or verbal environments,
and thus at least a noun class and a verb class as lexical form classes can be established.

An adjective class can be recognized (often as a subclass of stative verbs) based on the criterion of tropative usage.

An adverb class can also be recognized,
which contains forms appearing in clausal modifier positions (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.tam}).

In principle, function words can be introduced when introducing constructions licensing them.
In practice, for convenience, and considering grammaticalization, divergences in analyses,
and possible real-world divergence of their usages among speakers,
they are often assigned part of speech tags and introduced in a surface form-to-function manner.
An overview of these words (虛詞 in the traditional terminology) can be found in \citet[\citechap{4}]{li2004grammar}.

\subsection{Personal names}

Personal names in Classical Chinese have the surname-given name order.
Yet the meanings of both \term{surname} and \term{given name} are not always clear.
Before the Warring States period, the name of an aristocrat had two surnames:
姓 \form{xìng} \translate{ancestral clan name}, 氏 \form{shì} \translate{branch lineage name}.

The merger between the two was eventually finished during the Han dynasty,
possibly because of the collapse of the \form{fengjian} system:
people who were not members of the Zhou dynasty nobility became politically active,
and they likely had neither ancestral clan names nor branch lineage names.
It was common for them to adopt their birthplaces as their surnames,
which did not fit into the double surname system,
leading to the elimination of the distinction between \form{xìng} and \form{shì}.
Sima Qian himself does not clearly distinguish between the two.

\begin{exe}
    \ex 及生，名為政，姓趙氏
\end{exe}

Besides the surnames, people also have 名 \form{míng} \translate{given name},
the name given at birth.
Still it is considered impolite to use \emph{this} given name when having conversations with strangers or not-so-close friends:
in the latter cases, another name, the 字 \form{zì} \translate{courtesy name (lit. character)} is used.
A courtesy name, according to rites of Zhou,
was to be given at the adult ceremony of a man,
after which addressing him by his ``regular'' given name (i.e. his \form{míng}) was disrespectful.

\begin{exe}
    \ex 幼名，冠字
\end{exe}

A courtesy name may be formed by prefixation:
it was common before the Qin dynasty to first have a monosyllabic proper of the courtesy name
and then prefix 子 (a respectful title) or a numeric index (伯 \translate{first}, 仲 \translate{second}, \translate{叔} \translate{third}, \translate{季} \translate{last}) expressing the man's birth order in his family.

For example, an important disciple of Confucius, usually known as 子路 in the modern literature,
has 由 as his name given at birth (i.e. his \form{míng}):
子路 is his courtesy name, where 子 is a respectful title for a man.
In \form{Analects}, Confucius usually calls him 由 because of their close relationship,
while others, including the editors of \form{Analects}, call him 子路 (i.e. his \form{zì}).
His \form{shì} is 仲, and therefore when both the surname and the given name are called,
子路 is known as 仲由.

\begin{exe}
    \ex 子路聞之喜。子曰：「由也好勇過我，無所取材。」
    \ex 仲由可使從政也與
\end{exe}

The practice to have courtesy names rapidly ceased in the twentieth century,
when, quite similar to how the distinction between the ancestral clan name and the branch lineage name disappeared,
due to rapid modernization, scholar-officials lost their positions in government,
and rural land owners lost their properties both in mainland China and in Taiwan
due to violent or non-violent land reforms.
The ``lay'' citizens and peasants, now at the center stage of economy or even politics,
had never had serious exposure to the tradition of courtesy names,
and it is predictable that no one cared about courtesy names anymore.

Moreover, besides the courtesy name, certain people may have art names,
or 號 \form{hào} \translate{lit. mark}.
A person may have multiple art names.
The great poet 李白, whose courtesy name is 太白 \translate{lit. extreme-white},
had 青蓮居士 as his art name or \form{hào}.

\subsection{``Nouns used as verbs''}\label{sec:pos.verb.noun-to-verb}

The conventional term in Mandarin Chinese 名词作动词 \translate{nouns used as verbs} covers two phenomena,
corresponding to multiple functions and zero derivation \citep[\citesec{11.3}]{dixon2010basic2},
and also the rare case of ad hoc re-categorization of a root.

\subsubsection{Multiple functions}

Some roots have both nominal and verbal uses,
and there is usually some semantic connection between the interpretations of the two uses,
but this is not regularly inferable.
Here we consider some examples in \citet{yang1991dict}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item 楚 may mean \translate{the Chu state} or \translate{do what Chu people do}.
    \item 床 may mean \translate{bed} or \translate{settle down your bed or sleep on a bed}.
    \item 城 may mean \translate{city, castle} or \translate{build a city}.
\end{itemize}
The interpretation of the verbal usage is usually \emph{not} decided
from the meaning of the root and that the root is used in a verbal environment;
rather, it is instructed by the lexicon.
Therefore, the verbal usage of 城市 only means \translate{build a city}
although the \translate{do city-related things} reading in principle could make sense. 

Therefore, roots like 城, 楚 and 床 have double functions: nominal and verbal,
but the two functions are likely not related to each other by regular grammatical rules.
This corresponds to the ``multiple function'' case in \citet[\citesec{11.3}]{dixon2010basic2}.
Moreover, what is stored in the lexicon is not the bare, non-categorized root 城,
but one noun lexeme 城 \translate{city} that specifies its nominal usage 
and one verb lexeme \translate{build a city} that specifies its verbal usage,
and other seemingly possible ways to categorize the root, although attested elsewhere,
are ruled out by their absence in the lexicon.

The boundary between roots with double functions and roots undergoing zero derivation (see below) 
is somehow blurry,
as the nominal and verbal uses of 城 and 床 still seem to show a common pattern
and may be understood as a rare derivation.
This blurriness leads many grammatical works on Classical Chinese 
to simply refer to the two phenomena uniformly as ``nouns used as verbs''.

\subsubsection{Zero derivation}

In other cases the meaning of the verbal use of a root usually appearing in a nominal context
is regularly derived from the nominal meaning.
This is because although tropative or causative derivations in Classical Chinese
are mainly verb-to-verb,
they can also be applied to nouns.
In this way, from 臣 \translate{servant, official, minister}
we have the causative verbal usage \translate{make sb. dependent on},
and from 客 \translate{guest} we have the tropative usage \translate{consider sb. as a guest}.
These verbal usages are nothing different from noun-to-verb derivation observed in other languages,
so we regard the relevant phenomena as zero derivation as in \citet[\citesec{11.3}]{dixon2010basic2}.

In zero derivation, the meaning of the nominal usage has to be recorded in the lexicon,
the meaning of the verbal usage can be automatically decided from the derivation rule.
These derivations are however not completely regular and not for every word:
the lexicon also controls whether a derivational rule applies.

\subsubsection{Ad hoc re-categorization}

There are sporadic verbal usages of nouns that are almost never attested elsewhere,
like 军 in 沛公军霸上.
This means that ad hoc re-categorization of roots is possible in Classical Chinese,
and the meaning is to be decided from the context.
This is also possible in informal English%
\footnote{
    As in \form{I might [guinea pig] it for you.}
    A longer example is a historic event being \form{``Sappho and her friend'''d}.
} 
but usually not accepted in formal texts.
Alleged ad hoc categorized Classical Chinese roots are indeed a possibility, after all,
although their frequency is not high enough and cannot be exaggerated to be the norm rather than the exception.

\begin{exe}
    \ex 大楚兴，陈胜王
    \ex 然而不王者，未之有也
\end{exe}

the situation happening to it just happens,
and usually it does not have much control over it nor any volition to trigger it
(\citealt[\citepage{345}]{li2004grammar}; \citealt[\citepage{275}]{meiguang2018}).

In Classical Chinese, just like in other languages,
\category{become}/\category{be} verbs often have established causative usages,
forming \category{cause}-\category{become}/\category{be} argument structures
with the \term{causer} argument being the subject and the \term{theme} argument being internal
(\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument-structure.causative.synthetic}).
When a causer is absent, the structure of the clause 
is comparable to what sometimes is known as the middle voice in English
(e.g. \form{the door opened}; cf. the transitive \category{cause}-\category{become} \form{I opened the door}).
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.stative.1}) is an instance:
in its \category{cause}-\category{be} usage (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.causative.synthetic.2}),
the argument that is described as weak is an internal argument that appears after the verb,
but in (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.stative.1}),
the argument that is described as weak is the \emph{subject}:
the internal theme argument gets promoted to the subject position.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.stative.1} 秦强而赵弱
\end{exe}

The ``middle voice'' construction exemplified in (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.stative.1})
(known as 内动 in \citet{meiguang2018}) has a subject,
which corresponds to the argument that is the object in the \category{cause}-\category{become}/\category{be} construction
(i.e. the internal argument).



\subsubsection{The alternation between \category{be} and \category{become}}

Alternation between \category{be} and \category{become} verb frames is natural.
Some \category{become} verbs however do not have \category{be} counterparts.

\subsubsection{Impersonal stative verb}

It is possible for the argument of a stative verb to be \emph{not} promoted to the subject position.
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.stative.2}, \ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.stative.3}).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.stative.2}
    \gll 蝼蝈 鸣 \\
    \species{?} chirp \\
    \glt\translate{??? chirp.} (礼记·月令)

    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.stative.3}
    \gll [五 月]_{\text{temporal}} [鸣]_{\text{predicate}} [蜩]_{\text{internal argument}} \\
    five month chirp cicada \\
    \glt\translate{In the fifth (lunar) month, cicadas chirp.}
\end{exe}

(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.stative.3}) can be analyzed as a situation
if we assume that 鸣 is a \category{be} verb,
denoting a state where bugs continue to make noise,
and that the sole argument 蜩 remains in-situ and is not promoted to the subject position.

No other analysis is available: for instance a \category{do} verb can never have such a behavior
\citep[\citepage{351}]{meiguang2018}.



\section{Monotransitive constructions}

\subsection{Action verbs}
\label{sec:valency.simple.do}

The subject of a \category{do} clause usually has to be animate,
because it voluntarily initiates the event described by the clause
(\ref{ex:valency.simple.do.1}).
The subject is an \term{agent}, as opposed to a \term{causer} (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument-structure.causative})
or a \term{theme} (\prettyref{sec:valency.simple.state-and-change}).

\citet[\citepage{272}]{meiguang2018} lists some criteria
to distinguish a transitive \category{do} verb 
from a transitive \category{cause} verb
(\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument-structure.causative.synthetic}).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:valency.simple.do.1} 桓公杀公子纠
\end{exe}

\subsubsection{S=A labile verbs}\label{sec:valency.monotransitive.s-a}

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.argument.do-shift.transitive.1} 昭王反国
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.argument.do-shift.intransitive.1} 右师反
\end{exe}

\subsection{Causative verbs}

\citet[\citepage{272}]{meiguang2018} summarizes the main difference between transitive, \category{do} action verbs and causative verbs.

\subsubsection{S=P labile verbs}\label{sec:valency.monotransitive.s-p}

\begin{exe}
    \ex 大夫溃莒
    \ex 莒溃
\end{exe}

\subsubsection{Lexicalized synthetic causative construction}
\label{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument-structure.causative.fossilization}

Some \category{cause} verbs are fossilized,
and do not have clear intransitive counterparts
i.e. the un-causativized counterparts.
For instance, 伤 \translate{hurt} typically is a state transition verb meaning body, etc. being hurt,
and it also has a causative (i.e. \category{cause}-\category{become}) meaning
(\translate{make \dots hurt}).
The \category{cause}-\category{become} verb frame of 伤 however has gained a separate lexicalized meaning that can't be transparently inferred from the meaning of the \category{become} usage:
it can mean \translate{let \dots be damaged},
in which the object is not necessarily body or a person.
This usage of 伤 has no \category{become} or other intransitive counterpart.
The absence of a \category{become} counterpart can be proven by 
the ability for this fossilized figurative usage of 伤 to undergo ``passivization'' 
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.argument.causative.fossilize.1}),
which is otherwise not possible (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument-structure.pseudo-passive}).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.argument.causative.fossilize.1} 女红伤则寒之原也
\end{exe}

Verbs like 伤 in like (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.argument.causative.fossilize.1}) can easily be reanalyzed as \category{do} verbs.
This is likely a diachronic path of the creation of \category{do} verbs.
The verb 败 for example seems to be originally a \category{become} verb (\translate{to get corrupted})
and later gained a specific meaning of \translate{to defeat} in its \category{cause}-\category{become} usage,
which eventually evolved into a \category{do} usage
\citep[\citepage{285}]{meiguang2018}.

\subsubsection{Causative verbs with non-agentive subjects}
\label{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.simple.non-conventional-state}

Some verbs license subjects that look like arguments of prototypical \category{become} or \category{be} verbs:
the subject may be animate but it does not volitionally trigger the event.
The situation ``just happens to be the case'', and the subject can be described as a \term{theme} and not an \term{agent}.
What sets them apart from prototypical \category{become} or \category{be} verbs 
in \prettyref{sec:valency.simple.state-and-change}
is the fact that the subject seems quite unlike an internal argument.
In (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.non-conventional-theme.1}),
the subject 火 \translate{fire} is definitely a theme and not an agent:
the fire does not get to \emph{decide} if it burns the flag
\citep[\citepage{276}]{meiguang2018}.
Still the clause is not a prototypical \category{become} one
as there is an internal argument 其旗 in it,
and the theme 火 is an \emph{external} theme
\citep[\citepage{353}]{meiguang2018}.
These verbs therefore have difficulties participating in synthetic causativization
(\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument-structure.causative.synthetic}).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.non-conventional-theme.1} 火焚其旗
\end{exe}

\subsection{Experience verbs}
\label{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.simple.experience}
Some experience verbs, mostly verbs about emotions, behave like \category{become} verbs
\citep[\citepage{273}]{meiguang2018}:
when used as transitive verbs,
the subject does not look quite agentive and the clause is likely causative
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.simple.experience.2}),
and when used as intransitive verbs,
there is a clear internal change-of-state meaning
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.simple.experience.1}).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.simple.experience.1} 孔子成春秋，而乱臣贼子惧 (孟子·滕文公章句下)
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.simple.experience.2} 惧之以怒 (左传·昭公十三年)
\end{exe}

On the other hand, perception verbs (e.g. 见 \translate{look}) and cognition verbs (e.g. 知 \translate{know})
are often transitive,
and therefore are not compatible with the synthetic causative construction 
(\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument-structure.causative.synthetic};
\citealt[\citepage{274}]{meiguang2018}).
Intuitively, these verbs are \category{do}-like according to the criteria listed in \prettyref{sec:valency.simple.do}.
For instance, they can appear in 所 construction
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.simple.experience.do.1}).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.simple.experience.do.1} 異乎吾所聞
\end{exe}

Certain perception verbs however have developed a figurative, fossilized meaning,
and when intransitivized, can participate in synthetic causativization
(\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument-structure.causative.synthetic},
\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.causative.synthetic.3}).
This possibility indicates that these fossilized usages are \category{become}- or \category{be}-like:
见 \translate{meet formally} therefore means \translate{in the state of regularly meeting an important figure}.




\section{Experiential valency increasing}

\subsection{The affective constructions}

Classical Chinese has two affective constructions,
in which the subject is an experiencer suffering something bad from the situation described by the latter
\citep[\citepages{354-358}]{meiguang2018}.

The first affective construction simply attaches an experiencer to an argument structure
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.affective.1}).
In (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.affective.1.become}),
亡 appears as a \category{become} verb (\prettyref{sec:valency.simple.state-and-change}):
it is intransitive and its subject, the \work{Odes}, did not have control over its being ignored.
In (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.affective.1.affective-become}),
a new argument -- the experiencer subject -- is introduced to the argument structure of 亡:
the meaning of the sentence is \translate{the shepherds suffered from the sheeps getting lost.}

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.affective.1} 
    \begin{xlist}
        \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.affective.1.become} 亡 as a \category{become} verb
        \gll [\focus{诗}]_{\text{subject,theme: NP}} [\focus{亡}]_{\text{\category{become}}} 然后 春秋 作 \\
        \focus{poem} \focus{get.lost} then Spring-Autumn compose \\
        
        \glt\translate{The \work{Odes} got lost, and then the \work{Spring and Autumn Annals} was composed.}
        \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.affective.1.affective-become}

        \gll [\focus{二} \focus{人}]_{\text{subject: NP}} [相 与 牧 羊， 而 俱 [\focus{亡}]_{\text{\category{affective}-\category{become}}} \focus{其} \focus{羊}]_{\text{coordinated VP}} \\
        \focus{two} \focus{person} mutually go.together herd sheep \category{conj} all \focus{get.lost} \focus{\category{poss}} \focus{sheep} \\

\end{exe}

The second affective construction \emph{obligatorily} has an object,
which is in possession of the subject.
The meaning of (\ref{ex:valency.affective.type-2.1}), for example,

\chapter{Valency alternation}

\section{Causative constructions}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument-structure.causative}

A \term{causer} makes a situation to be the case,
but does not always do so intentionally.
It can therefore be inanimate,
as opposed to how an \term{agent} behaves
(\prettyref{sec:valency.simple.do}).

We can divide causative constructions in Classical Chinese into
synthetic and analytic ones.
In the synthetic causative construction,
there is only one verb in the surface form:
the causative valency alternation is supposedly marked by a prefix \form{*s-},
which is invisible in the written texts but is reflected by tonal changes of the verb.
If a root develops a lexicalized usage in the synthetic causative construction,
then \category{cause} verb is formed.

\subsection{Synthetic causative}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument-structure.causative.synthetic}

The synthetic causative construction applies to existing argument structures, or sometimes bare roots.
The synthetic causative construction cannot be applied to a \category{do} construction:
the reason is probably because a \category{do} construction is too ``big'',
already having a full-fledged wannabe subject \citep[\citepage{363-364}]{meiguang2018}.

On the other hand, the syntactic causative construction can be applied to 
a pseudo-passive structure (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.causative.synthetic.1})
and \category{become} or \category{be} (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.causative.synthetic.2}) argument structures.
Certain intransitivized experience verbs,
possibly having an argument structure comparable to a \category{become}/\category{do} verb (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.simple.experience}) with a wannabe subject also have causative usages
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.causative.synthetic.3}),
but their transitive counterparts are never compatible with the synthetic causative construction
\citep[\citepage{274}]{meiguang2018}.

Finally, the synthetic causative construction can be directly applied to a root (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.causative.synthetic.4}):
the word 妻 \translate{wife} is sometimes used as a verb,
meaning \translate{to marry daughter to \dots},
inconsistent with the meaning of (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.causative.synthetic.4}).
Therefore, in (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.causative.synthetic.4}),
妻 is ad hoc categorized into a \category{cause} verb,
its usual verbal usage being irrelevant here.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.causative.synthetic.1}
    \gll 是 夭 子蛮， 杀 御叔…… \\
    this die.young \category{name} kill \category{name} \\
    \glt\translate{This woman made Ziman die at a young age, and got Yushu killed\dots}
    
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.causative.synthetic.2}
    \gll 以 弱 天下 之 民 \\
    \category{purpose} weak world \category{gen} people \\
    \glt\translate{\dots to weaken the people.}

    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.causative.synthetic.3}
    \gll 子尾 见 疆 \\
    \category{name} formally.visit \category{name} \\
    \glt\translate{Ziwei let Jiang formally visit (with Xuanzi).} (左传·昭公二年)

    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.causative.synthetic.4} 妻帝之二女
\end{exe}

The labile S/O alternation between the \category{be}/\category{become} usage
and the \category{cause}-\category{be}/\category{become} usage
is quite regular in Classical Chinese;
verbs allowing this alternation are sometimes known as \term{ergative verbs}
\citep[\citepage{378}]{meiguang2018},
although the phenomenon is about the core argument structure and has nothing to do with ergativity in alignment.
It should be noted that not all \category{become}/\category{be} verbs are compatible
with the synthetic causative construction.
For instance, 鸣 \translate{chirp} in (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.verbal.stative.3})
does not have a transitive \category{cause}-\category{be} usage.
More examples are given in \citet[\citepage{276}]{meiguang2018}.
On the other hand, some clauses that look like \category{cause}-\category{be}/\category{become} clauses
actually do not have \category{be} or \category{become} counterparts 
(\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument-structure.causative.fossilization}).



\subsection{Analytic causative}\label{sec:valency.causative.analytic}

Classical Chinese has an analytic construction to express the causative meaning.
In (\ref{ex:valency.causative.analytic.1}),
the word 使 is applied to the stative structure 渚者居中原 \translate{people living on small lands reside inland},
meaning \translate{let people living on small lands in water live inland}.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:valency.causative.analytic.1}
    \gll 不 使 [渚 者]_{\text{shared object: \ac{np}}} [居]_{\text{\category{be}}} [中-原]_{\text{locative object: \ac{np}}} \\
    \category{neg} let small.land.in.water \category{nmlz} reside middle-land \\
    \glt\translate{\dots do not let people living on small lands in water live inland}
\end{exe}

The word 使 can be replaced by 令 (\ref{ex:valency.causative.analytic.2}) or 俾 (\ref{ex:valency.causative.analytic.3}) \citep[\citepage{376}]{meiguang2018}.
Note that in (\ref{ex:valency.causative.analytic.2}), 令 seems to be applied to a \category{do} argument structure.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:valency.causative.analytic.2} 令军勿敢犯
    \ex\label{ex:valency.causative.analytic.3} 俾民不迷
\end{exe}

A question is whether 使 is a lexical verb, or just a non-incorporated causative marker.
The fact that (\ref{ex:valency.causative.analytic.2}) involves a \category{do} verb
deviates from the behavior of the synthetic causative construction,
which is not compatible with \category{do} verbs (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument-structure.causative.synthetic}).
Further, the fact that we can choose among 使, 令 and 俾 is rather unusual for a grammatical marker:
we also note that 使 retains the meaning of \translate{send sb. to do sth.}
and 令 retains the meaning of \translate{command sb. to do sth.}
Therefore, we consider 使, 令 and 俾 to be \emph{lexical verbs},
and not grammaticalized causative markers.
What is grammaticalized is the causative clause with the form of V_1 NP VP,
with V_1 being one of these verbs.

\section{``Passive'' constructions}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument-structure.passive}

\subsection{The agent-less pseudo-passive construction}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument-structure.pseudo-passive}

What is often known as the passive in Classical Chinese is not really a passive construction
comparable to the English or Latin passive.
The main problem is the lack of a grammaticalized way to say the agent:
in a ``true'' passive construction, the original subject is somehow demoted
(represented by the appearance of \form{by} in English or the ablative case in Latin)
and sometimes omitted, and an internal argument is promoted to the subject position,
while the so-called ``passive'' constructions in Classical Chinese
-- which we name the \term{pseudo-passive} here --
involve an agent that is \emph{obligatorily removed} \citep[\citepage{287-289}]{meiguang2018}.

\subsubsection{Passivization vs. information packaging}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument-structure.pseudo-passive.information-packaging}

In a language that allows multiple topicalization and focalization,
whether omission of the agent is to be analyzed as argument structure alternation
instead of information packaging needs discussion.
One may want to analyze the promotion of the patient argument to the subject position 
as pro-drop of the agent plus topicalization of the patient.
This analysis essentially eliminates the necessity of the concept of subject in this construction.
However, we contend that this analysis is not viable.

First, we note that Classical Chinese has explicitly marked passive constructions like the 见-construction.
Their existence cannot be easily captured within an information structure-only framework.

Second, we have both passivization after causativization and causativization after passivization (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument-structure.pseudo-passive.causative-relation}),
and the semantics and the word order in the two constructions are regularly determined.
This strongly indicates that we are encountering valency alternation.

Third, information structure-driven omission of arguments, i.e. pro-drop in Classical Chinese mainly applies to arguments representing \emph{known} entities.
Yet the agent omitted in a pseudo-passive construction is frequently \emph{unknown}.

\subsubsection{Relation with causative constructions}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument-structure.pseudo-passive.causative-relation}

It is not frequent but not impossible to apply pseudo-passivization to a causative construction
\citep[\citepage{283}]{meiguang2018}.
Suppose we have a bivalence causative construction.
If pseudo-passivization is applied to it,
we are to suppress the external argument and let the internal argument to be the subject.
But such a bivalence causative construction usually has a \category{cause}-\category{become} structure,
and removing the causer leaves us a clause that looks just like a \category{become} clause.
So there are two competing analyses,
and since usually if a verb root is lexically licensed to head a \category{cause}-\category{become} clause,
then its usage in a \category{become} clause is also in the lexicon,
the simpler \category{become} analysis is preferred.
However, where this preference is eliminated, pseudo-passivization of a causative construction is possible 
(\citealt[\citepages{284,370-372}]{meiguang2018}; \prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument-structure.causative.fossilization}).

Given the strict conditions of pseudo-passivization \emph{after} causativization,
alternate application of pseudo-passivization and causativization
that results in a \category{pass}-\category{cause}-\category{pass}-\dots{} argument structure is not attested,
although such a combination is not semantically unfeasible (e.g. consider \translate{someone is made to be made to do sth.}),
and is occasionally attested, probably in a playful way, in living languages like modern Japanese
(\ref{ex:valency.passive.pseudo-passive.alternate-caus.japanese}).
This indicates that the argument structure in Classical Chinese is subject to
considerable lexical specification and valency alternation constructions are not completely productive.
In this particular case of the impossibility of a \category{caus}-\category{pass}-\category{caus} chain,
what is revealed probably also includes the decay of productive voice affixes in Old Chinese.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:valency.passive.pseudo-passive.alternate-caus.japanese} 見つかると削除させられさせられる
    \gll mitsukar-u to sakujo-sase-rare-sase-rare-ru \\
    see-\category{plain}.\category{nonpast} \category{cond} delete-\category{caus}-\category{pass}-\category{caus}-\category{pass}-\category{plain}.\category{nonpast} \\
    \glt\translate{If it is discovered, someone will be made to be made to delete it.} (Modern Japanese, from \href{https://kuroisi.com/blog/2253.html}{Internet}.)
\end{exe}

\subsubsection{The case with multiple internal arguments}\label{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument-structure.pseudo-passive.multiple-argument}

When the pseudo-passive construction is applied to double object clauses,
it is the \emph{recipient} that is promoted to the subject position 
(\ref{ex:valency.decrease.pseudo-passive.multi-valent.1}) \citep[\citepage{421}]{meiguang2018}.
On the other hand, when the pseudo-passive construction is applied to the corresponding prepositional argument construction,
it is the \emph{theme} that is promoted to the subject position
(\ref{ex:valency.decrease.pseudo-passive.multi-valent.2}).
These phenomena establish a hierarchy of \term{pivotality} of arguments (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.subject.argument-structure}).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:valency.decrease.pseudo-passive.multi-valent.1} 诸侯，赐弓矢然后征
    \ex\label{ex:valency.decrease.pseudo-passive.multi-valent.2} 药言先献于贵，然后闻于卑
\end{exe}

\subsection{The tense adverbs}\label{sec:tam.adverbs.tense}

嘗 seems to be a marker of the simple past.

The concept of future events can be expressed by 將,
but it is not compatible with 嘗.
On the other hand, in English, we have \form{would do sth.} or even \form{would have done sth.}
The incompatibility of 嘗 and 將 is evidence for the syntactic distinction between how future is expressed in English and in Classical Chinese:
in English, we have both a past/non-past distinction and a future/non-future distinction, beyond the standard Reichenbach system \citep{vikner1985reichenbach},
while in Classical Chinese, the past future tense is non-existent and we have a tripartite past/present (related to 矣; \prettyref{sec:tam.sfp.yi})/future distinction.

\section{The role of sentence final particles}\label{sec:tam.sfp}

\subsection{矣 and the tense system}\label{sec:tam.sfp.yi}

The appearance of the sentence final particle 矣 seems to have a tense effect.
When 矣 appears and there are no other \ac{tam} markers, the relation between the event time and the speech time is still largely arbitrary,
but the event always seems to have some relevance to the speech time:
the meaning of English \form{somebody did something} or \form{somebody had done something} is not admissible.
It seems that these observations can be summarized as follows:
the grammar of Classical Chinese does (although implicitly) recognize Reichenbach's speech time, reference time and action time,
and when 矣 appears, the latter two are identical, hence prohibiting the simple past meaning
 \citep[\citepages{437-446}]{meiguang2018}.
However, after considering its combinations with other markers, we do not consider 矣 itself to be a tense marker (\prettyref{sec:sfp.yi.not-tense}).

\chapter{Sentence final particles}

\section{Sentence final particles as discourse markers}

Classical sentence final particles have a variety of functions.
They may mark the interrogative force (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.sfp.interrogative.1}),
a judgmental meaning (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.sfp.judgement.1}),
and aspectual values (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.sfp.aspectual.1}).

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.sfp.interrogative.1} 大车无輗，小车无軏，其何以行之哉
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.sfp.judgement.1} 人而无信，不知其可也
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.sfp.aspectual.1} 温故而知新，可以为师矣
\end{exe}

It seems a sentence final particle can be shared by two conjuncts.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.sfp.judgement.2} 虎者，戾虫；人者，甘饵也
\end{exe}

In early texts, a sentence final particle can be inserted after the main verb.

\begin{exe}
    \ex 巧言令色，鲜矣仁
\end{exe}

At first glance, the sentence final particle 矣 marks the perfect aspect
(\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.force.sentential-aspect.yi.1}),
while 也 is for clauses describing something happening regularly, not a single concrete event (\ref{ex:grammatical.clause.force.sentential-aspect.ye.1})
\citep[\citepages{443-445}]{meiguang2018}.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.force.sentential-aspect.yi.1} 余助苗长矣
    \ex\label{ex:grammatical.clause.force.sentential-aspect.ye.1} 將发命也
\end{exe}

矣 and 也, however, are different from prototypical aspect markers in several aspects.
First, 矣 appears predominantly in direct quotations in Classical texts,
which suggests that it has conversational functions.
也 frequently appears in narratives as a part of the judgmental construction
(\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.topic}),
which lacks \ac{tam} marking, and 也 cannot be a prototypical aspect marker in that context.
Second, it seems that 矣 and 也 can be shared by two coordinated conjuncts with different subjects,
which is rather unusual for an aspect marker.

Therefore, the grammatical category corresponding to 矣 and 也, whatever it is,
is not a typical \ac{tam} category,
and hence we disagree with \citet{meiguang2018}'s analysis.
Its scope is wider than \ac{tam} categories:
a \ac{tam} category is in relation with a nucleus clause,
while 矣 and 也 are in relation with a sentence,
i.e. an arbitrarily complex clause that is one utterance in a conversational context.
This is consistent with the usual analysis of sentence final particles 
in modern Standard Mandarin
\citep{paul2014particles,pan2021sentence}.

\begin{exe}
    \ex 亦各言其志也已矣
\end{exe}

\begin{infobox}{Alternative analysis}{sfp-alternative-analysis}
    \citet[\citepage{233}]{zhudexigrammar} acknowledges the widespread analysis that sentence final particles are in relation with the whole sentence, not the nucleus clause,
    but insists that certain sentence final particles are a part of the predicate.
    Yet no convincing argument is provided.
    Among the three distributional classes he recognizes in Mandarin,
    two (marking the interrogative/imperative force, and attitude of the speaker)
    are uncontroversially attached to \emph{sentences} and not nucleus clauses.
    The remaining class, which is called the ``tense'' class by \citet{zhudexigrammar}
    and is structurally the innermost,
    resembles the class of 矣 and 也 discussed here,
    seems to be forbidden in most embedded clauses \citep{deng2010},
    just like the other two classes do.
    Therefore all the three classes of Mandarin sentence final particles described in \citet{zhudexigrammar}
    are indeed in relation with the sentence and not the nucleus clause,
    which is consistent with the structural status of sentence final particles in Classical Chinese.
    
    We also note that it is not completely impossible for two independent nucleus clauses to share one \ac{tam} marker.
    In Japhug, for example, a series of nucleus clauses with different subjects can be coordinated with the \ac{tam} categories being marked at the end of the compound clause \citep[\citepages{1090-1091}]{jacques2021grammar}.
    However, Japhug lacks clear clause-level subject
    (\citealt[\citesec{2.5.3}]{jacques2021grammar}),
    and therefore coordinated nucleus clauses with a shared \ac{tam} marker but different ``subjects'' is less strange in Japhug than it is in Classical Chinese:
    in the latter, we have a well-defined clausal pivot grammatical relation (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.subject.clause-pivot}) whose scope is over all \ac{tam} categories,
    making clauses sharing the \ac{tam} marker but not subjects rather unusual,
    but in the former this is probably not the case.

    \citet[\citepages{443-445}]{meiguang2018} relies solely on semantic criteria.
    Although we do not believe his analysis of 也 and 矣 as tense markers is completely correct,
    the two clearly have non-trivial interaction with \ac{tam} categories,
    a phenomenon also observed in Mandarin,
    where the lowest sentence final particle has access to \ac{tam} categories of the nucleus clause \citep[\citepage{258}]{paul2014new}.
\end{infobox}

\section{矣}

\paragraph*{矣 is not a tense marker}\label{sec:sfp.yi.not-tense}

Besides the fact that sentence final particles are discourse-oriented and therefore not prototypical \ac{tam} markers,
we also note the following two facts.

First, 矣 is compatible with both 將 and 嘗 (\ref{ex:tam.sfp.yi.future.1}, \ref{ex:tam.sfp.yi.past.1}; \prettyref{sec:tam.adverbs.tense}), which probably means it is not directly related to the tense system.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:tam.sfp.yi.future.1} 難將至矣
    \ex\label{ex:tam.sfp.yi.past.1} 牛山之木嘗美矣
\end{exe}

Second, 矣 seems to have a weak exclamative function.
For example, in (\ref{ex:tam.sfp.yi.attitude.1}),
the speaker has something to say but has waited for quite a while for a change to say it.
In (\ref{ex:tam.sfp.yi.attitude.2}),
Confucius is impressed by how well cultivated 韶乐 \translate{\form{Shao} music} is.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:tam.sfp.yi.attitude.1} 吾欲言之久矣
    \ex\label{ex:tam.sfp.yi.attitude.2} 尽美矣
\end{exe}

We therefore conclude that 矣 is not a tense marker,
and instead analyze it as a ``sentential aspect'' marker,
which informs the listener that a new piece of information arrives.
This is consistent with the function of the sentence final 了 in modern Standard Chinese.

\begin{infobox}{Alternative analysis}{yi-alternative-analysis-tense}
    In \citechap{11} of \citet{meiguang2018}, it is claimed that 矣 is a prototypical tense marker.
    We agree with \citet{meiguang2018} in that Classical Chinese has a (rather weak) tense system, and we basically reiterate his arguments above.
    His arguments for the claim that 矣 itself is a tense marker however we do not consider valid.
    On \citepage{447}, he argues that 矣 appears in imperative sentences and therefore cannot be related to aspectual categories.
    But the category of tense also rarely appears in imperative sentences.
    On the other hand, the sentential aspect category is essentially a discourse-level category, and has no inherent conflict with the imperative speech act.
    On \citepage{448}, he notes that in coordination, when 矣 appears at the end of the first clause, it sets up a reference time for the following clauses.
    Yet he states clearly that the reference time is actually the event time of the first clause,
    whose relation with the speech time is \emph{not} specified by 矣.

    On the other hand, if we accept the analysis that 矣 hints at new information that the speaker wants to express to the listener at the speech time,
    then we can easily understand why 矣,
    because a generic fact like \form{the Earth goes around the Sun} rarely is new information,
    and a piece of new information usually involves a specific situation at a specific time,
    which is inevitably compared with the speech time, and hence the tense system.
    The fact that 矣 is often related to the present tense or the present perfect tense 
    may simply be because of the absence of other \ac{tam} markers:
    cf. (\ref{ex:tam.sfp.yi.future.1}, \ref{ex:tam.sfp.yi.past.1}).
\end{infobox}

\chapter{Theoretical framework}\label{chap:intro.theory}


{\small
The theoretical framework of this work is strongly inspired by Distributed Morphology (for an introduction see \citet{siddiqi2009syntax}) plus Cartographic Syntax (e.g. \citet{cinque1999adverbs}),
although I intentionally choose to reuse the terminology in descriptive grammars.
We will see that the structuralist tradition of Chinese linguistics agrees incredibly well with the generative framework.

\section{Lexicon, grammar, wordhood}

The architecture of a language is assumed to be in line with the basic assumptions of Distributed Morphology. 
The so-called ``lexicon'' consists of a list of roots (List A),
each of which is only realizable in combination with grammatical formatives (corresponding to certain syntactic positions) (List B),
and grammatical objects of arbitrary sizes with custom meanings (List C).

List B dictates grammatical lexicalization i.e. morphosyntactic listedness of grammatical properties of forms
(i.e. subcategorization; cf. \prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument-structure.causative.fossilization}),
which is related to wordhood (\prettyref{sec:theory.wordhood}).
We note that this does not entail the necessity of a lexicalist grammatical framework where structure building mechanisms for words and phrases are categorically different (cf. \citealt{bruening2018lexicalist}).

List C dictates idiomization, which may also be known as semantic lexicalization (we avoid using the term \term{lexicalization} to denote idiomization in this work).
The distinction between (syntactic) lexicalization and (semantic) idiomization is discussed in e.g. \citet{mcinnerney2023argument}.

Lexicalization and/or idiomization of a complex structure may eventually lead to the collapse of its internal makeup, causing (\emph{syntactic}) fossilization.
An example is the collapse of verbs taking complement clauses into compound verbs.
Fossilization may end up producing a single \emph{root},
of which only diachronic analysis is possible.
Syntactic fossilization is important in Classical Chinese due to its long history.

Parts of speech labels hold different meanings in different contexts.
The noun may just be defined as the head word of a noun phrase; 
hence notions of ``verbs used as nouns''.
Alternatively, people refer to \form{form classes} in the lexicon that share similar lexicalized properties as parts of speech.%
\footnote{
    This obviously begs the question: where does the rigidity of these form classes come from?
    Why can't, say, a Latin noun have no gender feature?
    This may be explained by cross-linguistic variances in List A:
    we may assume that the nominal categorizer is gendered \citep{kramer2015gender}.
    Alternatively this may be explained by a language-specific well-formedness condition.
    The competition between the two analyses is a part of the wider macroparameter vs. microparameter debate, which we do not touch in this work.
}

This second definition of parts of speech sees more cross-linguistic variances.
To say that the Latin word \form{canis} is a noun
means that \form{canis} belongs to a form class in which every member  
usually appear as the head of an \ac{np},
carries an inherent gender feature and a number feature,
and that its inflection pattern follows one of Latin nominal declensions.
(It's easy to imagine how such a form looks like in Distributed Morphology.)
To say that \form{dog} is an English noun means something else:
it means the root \form{dog} regularly appears in the nominal modification construction (as in \form{a [dog]_{\text{nominal (not \ac{np})}} tag}),
which is not attested in Latin.

\section{High-level constituents; grammatical relations}

The analyses in \prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause} and \prettyref{sec:grammatical.np} are inspired by the extended CP and DP structures proposed in Cartography.
To avoid confusion caused by technical terms in generative syntax,
I intentionally use terms like \term{sentence}, \term{nucleus clause} and \term{argument structure} in place of CP, TP and vP.
Similarly we talk about \term{noun phrases} instead of DPs.

Concepts like SpecTP and SpecvP, in a descriptive context, correspond to concepts like \term{clausal subject} and \term{pivot in the argument structure} (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.subject}).
Structural positions definable at the vP level we call argument slots below
(the much more frequently used term in generative syntax is thematic roles; the correspondence between traditionally semantically motivated thematic role classification and the light verb shell has been extensively discussed, as in the literature on UTAH),
and structural positions definable at the TP level we call \term{grammatical functions}.
The ``core'' of CPs and DPs (i.e. the roots at their centers plus the categorizers) -- instead of the grammatical markers -- should be known as heads. 

In CPs and DPs recurring patterns or \term{constructions} and \term{grammatical relations} in them can be observed:
subject-predicate and verb-object relations,
subordination and coordination structures.%
\footnote{
    Here the term is used \emph{without} the implication 
    that a construction is somehow understood as a whole 
    and its internal structures should not be further analyzed,
    contrary to the fundamental hypothesis of e.g. various Construction Grammars.
}
The fine details of the light verb shell I replace by concepts like \term{\category{do} clause} or \term{\category{cause} clause} (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.verbal.argument.do-be-become}).

Grammatical markers can be distinguished from content roots and words in that 
grammatical markers are from a closed class and mark grammatical categories and functions in certain constructions, and have no meaning outside these constructions.
This often means that forms containing grammatical markers that seemingly semantically make sense are not acceptable (e.g. \prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.tam}).

The correspondence between descriptive notations and theoretical linguistic concepts has been demonstrated in \citet{deng2010},
which shows that Minimalist generative syntax and the constituency-based analysis seen in the Chinese linguistics tradition outlined in \prettyref{sec:introduction.previous.modern}, which appears strongly inspired by American structuralism exemplified in \citet{cgel}, are compatible to each other,
with the former being a more concise form of the latter 
and the latter being a logical consequence of the former under certain assumptions.%
\footnote{
    Note that \citet{deng2010}, \citet{cgel} and works in \prettyref{sec:introduction.previous.modern} are all lexicalist, which we have argued is not necessary to account for phenomena purportedly supporting the lexicalist hypothesis.
    These works are also based on the more traditional X-bar theory:
    the syntactic differences between the subject and the object, for example,
    are explained by the difference between specifier and complement.
    In Cartography, what is recognized as a complement may be a lower specifier:
    thus [V O]_{\text{V'}} is replaced by a ``transitivity phrase'' [O [\category{trans} √V]_{\text{\category{trans}'}}]_{\text{\category{trans}P}} \citep{siddiqi2009syntax},
    where the category-less verb root is first combined with a transitivity functional head and then combined with the object.
    The resulting tree diagram is quite like the X-bar scheme before, say, the DP hypothesis:
    the only difference being that there are many X'-like projections, all of which may appear in other constructions.
    For instance, the English \term{nominal} mentioned in \citet{cgel}, which is often recognized as N', can actually be a modifier in the DP,
    as in \form{a [Fuji apple] tree}.
    \citet{deng2010} shows that the subject-predicate structure or the verb-object structure can all be derived from the traditional X-bar scheme:
    the Cartographic approach pushes this decomposition even further and demonstrates that the traditional X-bar scheme itself can be further decomposed into a series of functional heads and roots.
} 
Further, as shown in \prettyref{sec:theory.dependency},
the constituency-based analyses are consistent with the Basic Linguistic Theory (the de facto unified framework in modern linguistic description of underdocumented languages).

The three approaches can be seen as three ``representations'' of the same grammatical complexity class,
and their differences are mostly notational.%
\footnote{
    A more important controversy is the mental status of grammar.
    Generativism treats grammar as a semi-autonomous component of human cognitive abilities.
    But it has been argued that grammatical constructions originate from domain-general cognitive abilities, and there is actually no such thing as an autonomous mental grammar.
    Detailed discussions on this topic are far beyond the scope of this work,
    and can only be finally settled down with the assistance of neurological studies.
    Here we just note that currently no comprehensive description of a language has been successfully attempted under this line of thinking.
    On the contrary, in physicists' terms, the grammatical framework adopted here is at least a good \term{effective theory}.
    So-called functional-typological grammars do not differ significantly in the way they carry out analyses from structuralist works.

    Another topic is whether natural language-like ``grammars'' appear in systems that are demonstrably independent to the human neurological linguistic capacity,
    which may also challenge the domain-specific status of the latter.
    This is partly discussed in \prettyref{sec:writing-system.theoretical}.
    Note however that such discussions are largely irrelevant to linguistic description,
    for reasons mentioned above.
}
Which framework to use is determined by the properties of the language.
For instance, although the definition of the verb phrase does not alter the grammar system substantially,
Dixon's definition works more smoothly for a language with a lot of auxiliaries but rather infrequent subject-sharing coordinations.
It turns out that for most constructions, the method in \citet{cgel} is a good choice for Classical Chinese.

We note that we do not consider adjunction an independent structure-building process that is different from Merge.
A detailed discussion can be found in sections on the argument/adjunct distinction \prettyref{chap:grammatical}.

\section{Dependency}\label{sec:theory.dependency}

Grammars can be written in terms of \emph{dependency relations}, instead of \emph{constituency relations}.
The two however are largely equivalent \citep{boston2009dependency}.
In his Basic Linguistic Theory, \citet{dixon2009basic} fervently argues against constituency analysis (and also other aspects of generative syntax) and advocates for a ``flat'' constituency structure, possibly with the levels of clause and noun phrase only,
where the rest of the grammatical information is represented by dependency relations 
(e.g. \citealt[\citepage{49}]{dixon2009basic}).
Yet the primacy of noun phrases and clauses in syntax is indeed acknowledged by generative syntax (for instance, they are \term{phases}; it is also not uncommon to see terms like \term{CP syntax} or \term{DP syntax} in generative works).

The binary constituency relations in generative syntax and in American structuralism are not arbitrary and have consequences.
For instance, when applied to the argument structure,
they are related to properties of arguments in valency alternation (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.subject.argument-structure}),
and the subject-predicate binary division is directly related to the clausal pivotal status of the subject.
These phenomena of course have to be taken into account by Basic Linguistic Theory,
and labels like ``clausal pivot'' and ``surface S, deep O'' have to be attached to dependency arcs between the verb and the arguments,
essentially labeling the ``distance'' between the two. 
As a parallel, the ancient Indian grammarian Pāṇini initially proposes a grammatical framework in which arguments are all equal, but later commentators still effectively set up a pivot position in the argument structure.%
\footnote{
    The main difference between Pāṇini's treatment of Sanskrit and modern linguistic theories
    is that Pāṇini apparently treats all dependency relations equally
    and there is, for example, no concept of the pivot or the ``external argument'' of a clause.
    This is however modified in the commentaries on his \work{Aṣṭādhyāyī},
    which explicitly allow an argument to be promoted to the agent position
    because of the intentions of the speaker \citep{keidan2017subjecthood}.
    The agent position thus becomes a subject position at least in the argument structure, consistent with modern practices (\prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause.subject.argument-structure}).

    The Paninian tradition therefore is extremely close to modern linguistic description practice;
    the most important difference probably is that
    modern linguistic description, practically, may even be less rigorous than \work{Aṣṭādhyāyī},
    because of possible competing ``mind grammars'' among speakers with mutual intelligibility
    or even within the mind of one speaker,
    and also the fact that a description as detailed as \work{Aṣṭādhyāyī}
    requires corpus data whose quality and quantity exceed the capacity of most field linguists.
}

Other phenomena that can be attributed to binary constituent structures include
ordering of tense and aspect auxiliaries and adverbs à la Cinque, attested in languages completely unrelated to the Germanic or Romance languages that have been thoroughly investigated in Cartography (e.g. \citealt[\citepages{166-167}]{grimm2021grammar}),
and also available clause sizes: it is rare that in a language with tense, aspect and modality marking,
there exists a clause type with discourse devices but no tense, aspect and modality marking,
which can be explained simply by the fact that CP is built on TP.

\section{On the term \term{phrase}}

Besides the theoretical problems outlined above, there are also some minor, largely notational inconsistencies between the grammatical theories mentioned here.
One such inconsistency is the definition of the \term{phrase}.
\citet{dixon2009basic} calls the main verb plus auxiliaries -- without any argument -- the \term{verb phrase}.
This notion of verb phrase has a generative syntax counterpart: the CP phase.
In a CP, arguments are phases themselves,
and the tense, aspect and modality categories marked by the auxiliaries are thus in some senses closer to the main verb.
On the other hand, the term \term{verb phrase} generally means the verb plus internal arguments in constituency-based analyses (as in e.g. \citealt{danckaert2017development}).

\section{Wordhood}\label{sec:theory.wordhood}

Another problem is the definition of \term{word}.
Since we reject the lexicalist hypothesis,
we need to distinguish phonological wordhood
from morphological wordhood (where the boundary of the morphological template is the boundary of the morphological word).

We first note that it is not possible to define wordhood purely on (abstract) syntactic structures.
We can define a word to be a very small constituent:
if it is impossible to infer the argument structure of a compound verb in a given language,
then we conclude that the two branches of the compound are not categorized,
and therefore the compound is a rather small constituent and hence a word.
But in this way \form{sinned} is \emph{not} a syntactic word as it involves a clausal category (i.e. the past tense).
For \form{sinned} to be a syntactic word, syntactic wordhood can be based on Dixon's verb phrase,
and inevitably \form{have been eating} is a syntactic word.

Therefore, (abstract) properties of structure building processes cannot be the only criterion used in defining wordhood.%
\footnote{
    This is to be contrasted with the criteria distinguishing compounds and nominal complementation in \citet{cgel},
    which does rely heavily on abstract properties of perceived word-like and phrase-like structure building processes.
}

Morphological wordhood, i.e. grammatical wordhood,
corresponds to post-syntactic aggregation of roots and formatives in Distributed Morphology, guided by List B.
Its traces can be found in existence of morphological templates that can't be motivated by purely combinatorial syntax,
boundaries of forms in phonology that lack pure prosodic motivation, etc.
Further, the way List B works means wordhood is strongly related to lexical specification of grammatical behaviors,
as the inability for a transitive verb to have no object is attributed to its stem cannot be spelt out as a grammatical word without a transitivity head (see \citealt{siddiqi2009syntax} for details).
Therefore, the lexicon (understood as List B) is often said to contain \term{words} or \term{lexemes} (the latter term assumes a clear inflection/derivation distinction; see below).
But note that a form with lexicalized grammatical properties may only be a half-word,
like English neo-classical combining form \form{geo-} (which prefers an adjective-like position but is not a word).

The distinction between derivation and phrasal grammar follows from the definition of word.
Ideally, the direction of inference should not be reversed:
a structure building process being structurally limited in complexity (e.g. impossibility of coordination) should not automatically qualify it as a word-internal process and hence a derivation process (though in the existing literature, the derivation/phase grammar distinction is often dealt in this way).
These structural criteria are also rarely decisive:
the form \form{pre- and post-revolutionary} obviously mixes phrasal coordination with affixation, but the latter is prototypically derivational.%
\footnote{
    In this concrete example, we consider \form{pre-revolutionary} a grammatical word 
    because phonologically the boundary between \form{pre-} and \form{revolutionary} doesn't matter:
    the form is read as a single phonological word and there is a single stress.
    This is hard to explain if there are two grammatical words in it.
}

The derivation/inflection distinction involves multiple parameters that do not always coincide.
The distinction may be defined based on abstract structural criteria
(thus tense marking is inflectional while argument structure alternation is perhaps not);
this inevitably has to rely on a pre-defined concept of wordhood
(or otherwise auxiliary verb constructions have to be considered inflectional; cf. the same problem faced in syntactic definition of wordhood).
The distinction sometimes just means the synchronic/diachronic distinction:
derivation processes are supposed to be less productive,
a criterion largely consistent with the structural criterion 
(as a smaller grammatical unit also tends to fossilize faster),
but productive derivation processes exist.
The most uncontroversial definition of the derivation/inflection distinction relies on the morphological template of words,
when the inflectional ending can be relatively clearly set apart from the stem
(this, though, means stem alternation marking of aspect in Latin is derivational).




}

The problem of \prettyref{fig:underline-example} is also its compactness:
the label of a constituent cannot be too long, or otherwise the whole diagram cannot be fit within one line.
In this work, we represent examples in a way that is more aligned to modern description grammars:
we place constituents in brackets, and then label these brackets by subscripts.
(\ref{ex:introduction.theory.format.1}) is an example.
When we have grammatical markers,
we do not put them (e.g. the sentence final particle 矣 in \ref{ex:introduction.theory.format.2}) in brackets to highlight the lexical/functional distinction,
consistent with the suggestion of \citet[\citepage{49}]{dixon2009basic}.

\begin{exe}
    \ex\label{ex:introduction.theory.format.1} 
    \gll [[季氏]_{\text{subject:NP}} [[將]_{\text{\category{tam} adverb}} [伐 顓臾]_{\text{core VP}}]_{\text{predicate:VP}}]_{\text{verbal clause}} \\
    Ji-family.branch.lineage.name will  subdue Zhuanyu \\
    \glt\translate{Jishi is going to invade Zhuanyu.} \hfill (\work{Analects})
    
    \ex\label{ex:introduction.theory.format.2}
    \gll [[[民_{\text{subject,A:NP}} [鮮 ---_{\text{P}}]_{\text{predicate:VP}}]_{\text{subject: complement clause}} [久]_{\text{predicate:AdjP}}]_{\text{verbal nucleus clause}} 矣_{\text{sentential aspect}}]_{\text{sentence}} \\
    people lack {} long \category{sfp} \\

One further issue is phonological transcription of the texts.
The standard practice is to apply the Modern Mandarin phonology (romanized in \form{pinyin}),
following the reading tradition \citep{pulleyblank1995outline};
this is comparable to how ancient Greek texts are read in modern Greece.
Historically, it was also possible to read Classical Chinese with different phonological systems.

This standard practice does not give us much insights into the phonological reality of these texts.
Some choose to transcribe texts in reconstructed Old Chinese \citep{jacques2022nature}.
Details in reconstruction of the phonology of Old Chinese remains a highly debated topic,
and no mutually accepted lossless romanization that reflects the historical phonology exists currently.

\section{Procedure of analysis}

We adopt a top-down order in writing this book.
We start with high-level constituents, i.e. clauses and noun phrases.
Grammatical systems are identified when these forms are analyzed,
and are introduced in the rest of the sections of \prettyref{sec:grammatical.clause} and \prettyref{sec:grammatical.np}.
Parts of speech divisions are made based on grammatical evidence and not semantics.

This organization is in contrast with some descriptive grammars,
which start with e.g. the nominal and verbal morphology,
and then discuss grammatical relations like possession, modification, and argument structure,
and then outline the structure of the simple clause and complex clauses.
This is not the approach taken here,
partly because Classical Chinese is a rather analytic language and there is simply no inflectional morphology to describe,
and partly because a well organized study of its grammar has to have the whole syntactic environment in mind:
otherwise it will likely collapse into a dictionary of the various usages of function words.

This work also pays attention to the combinatorial possibilities of dependents in clauses and noun phrases and their relative orders and scope,
which is often skipped in \emph{ethno-philological} works mainly aiming to prepare the reader to comprehend \emph{natural texts} in the language in question.
We aim to study the grammar of Classical Chinese as a machine that takes lexical items and produces \emph{arbitrarily} complex utterances,
and to understand the structure of this machine.
The approach is admittedly not unproblematic for a dead language,
which has no native speaker with acceptability intuitions and may also have strong internal variances historically.
